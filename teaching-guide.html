Course Pacing

The course is built around five delivery days and eight teaching strands, visible on the course hub's progress strip. Each day groups two or three lessons that share a strand, so the pacing table below is also a strand map — useful for deciding where to slow down or where a cohort can move faster.

DayLessonsStrand(s)Suggested Schedule
Day 1Lesson 1 – Lesson 3FoundationsFull day. Lesson 1 (convergence) runs longest — it resets assumptions learners arrive with; leave extra time for Section 03's misconceptions to surface here.
Day 2Lesson 4 – Lesson 5ProtocolsFull day, two longer sessions. Lesson 5's NMEA/AIS framing benefits from a slower walk-through before the Lab 1 hands-on exercise.
Day 3Lesson 6 – Lesson 7ThreatsFull day. Both lessons carry a "case study only / no live exploitation" safety flag — reinforce the Safeguards policy boundary before starting.
Day 4Lesson 8 – Lesson 9Frameworks · GovernanceFull day. Denser reading load — the four Core Framework pages support Lesson 8 and can be assigned as pre-reading for a faster in-person pace.
Day 5Lesson 10 – Lesson 12Identity & Access · Operations · CapstoneLongest day. Lesson 12's capstone needs protected working time — consider splitting Day 5 across two shorter sessions if your cohort's schedule allows it.

This pacing assumes roughly four contact hours per lesson including discussion and any paired lab or simulator time. A cohort with less contact time per day can spread the same 12 lessons across more than five calendar days without changing the strand grouping.

02

Prerequisite Check

This course does not assume any maritime background — Lesson 1 through Lesson 3 build that from the ground up. What it does assume is a working, general familiarity with networking concepts. A short diagnostic before Day 1 catches gaps early, when they're cheap to fill.

Suggested pre-course diagnostic (5–8 questions, ungraded)

  1. 1

    IP addressing and subnets. Can the learner explain, in plain terms, what an IP address and a subnet are for? This underlies Lesson 4's VLAN and segmentation content.

  2. 2

    Client/server and request-response. A basic sense of how two devices exchange data is enough — Lesson 4 and Lesson 5 build protocol-specific detail on top of this.

  3. 3

    Ports and protocols at a high level. Not memorized port numbers — just the idea that different services use different, identifiable channels.

  4. 4

    What a firewall does. The general concept of a boundary that allows or blocks traffic, which Lesson 7's segmentation countermeasures and the zone model from Lesson 2 both build on directly.

  5. 5

    Comfort reading a simple diagram. Learners who can follow a basic network diagram will move faster through the zone-model and architecture material in Lessons 2, 4, and 8.

A learner who is shaky on two or more of these is not disqualified from the course — flag it so the instructor can plan a short Day 1 refresher, rather than letting the gap surface mid-lesson.

03

Common Misconceptions

Learners arrive with mental models built from general IT security or from popular coverage of maritime incidents, and both produce predictable misconceptions. Naming them early — ideally on Day 1 — headed off a lot of confusion in later lessons.

Why learners believe it: the mental image of a ship at sea, disconnected from shore, is decades out of date but persistent.

How to counter it: Lesson 1's VSAT convergence content is the direct rebuttal — satellite links, crew connectivity, and shore-side remote access have merged vessel networks with the wider internet on most modern fleets. Have learners trace one concrete path from a crew laptop to a navigation system before this misconception gets challenged in the abstract.

Why learners believe it: AIS looks and behaves like a reliable positioning feed on a chart display, so it reads as equally authoritative.

How to counter it: Lesson 5's protocol framing and Lesson 6's spoofing case studies together show that AIS messages are self-reported and unauthenticated by design — anyone transmitting on the frequency can put a fabricated vessel on the chart. Neither AIS nor unencrypted civilian GPS carries strong built-in authentication.

Why learners believe it: a learner coming from general ICS/OT security sees PLCs, sensors, and control loops and assumes the discipline transfers unchanged.

How to counter it: Lesson 2's SOLAS and ISM Code content shows a governance layer — safety of life at sea, flag-state and port-state oversight, classification society survey — with no direct equivalent in a factory. A vessel is also a mobile asset that changes jurisdiction, crew, and connectivity constantly; a shore plant does not.

Why learners believe it: routine patch cadence is second nature in general IT, so the delay in applying a fix elsewhere reads as negligence rather than constraint.

How to counter it: Lesson 7's dry-dock-length patch window content shows that many shipboard systems can only be safely updated during scheduled yard periods, sometimes a year or more apart — which is exactly why compensating controls (segmentation, allowlisting, jump hosts) carry so much weight in maritime OT.

Why learners believe it: air-gap thinking is a reasonable first instinct, and it's not entirely wrong — it's incomplete.

How to counter it: Lesson 6's insider-risk and supply-chain content covers vendor USB drives, contractor laptops during a port call, and OEM remote-support sessions — none of which require the target system to have its own internet connection. An isolated network is safer, not safe.

04

Discussion Prompts

Open-ended prompts, one or two per strand, useful for small-group discussion, a virtual breakout, or a warm-up before the lesson's quiz.

Foundations

What changed on a vessel's network the day its first VSAT terminal went live — and who, if anyone, was responsible for securing that new path?

Foundations

Why might "24/7 uptime" mean something different, and stricter, for a ship's steering system than it does for a shore data center?

Protocols

NMEA 0183 and AIS were designed decades before maritime cybersecurity was a discipline. Should they be replaced, retrofitted with authentication, or defended entirely at the network layer instead?

Threats

A ransomware operator hits a port's business network, not its terminal operations network. Why might they still cause an operational shutdown?

Threats

Given the dry-dock patch window problem, is "patch everything quickly" ever the right top priority for a maritime OT security program?

Frameworks & Governance

If you could only require one of IACS UR E26/E27, the IMO instrument, NIST SP 800-82, or IEC 62443 for a fleet operator with limited resources, which would you pick, and what would it fail to cover?

Identity & Access · Operations

A vendor needs remote access to an ECDIS over a satellite link mid-voyage. What's the minimum set of controls that makes that access acceptable rather than merely convenient?

Operations · Capstone

What does "contain the incident" mean on a ship that cannot simply be unplugged and is three days from the nearest port?

06

Facilitation Tips

  1. 1

    Run the per-lesson quiz live when you can. The in-browser quizzes give immediate feedback (see the Assessment & Rubric Bank) — projecting the results screen and discussing a missed question in the moment lands better than assigning it as homework.

  2. 2

    Use the mapping activities as a mid-lesson checkpoint, not just an end-of-lesson wrap-up — the zone-mapping and framework-mapping exercises surface confusion earlier, while there's still time in the session to address it.

  3. 3

    Let learners drive the toggle-based simulations themselves rather than demonstrating from the front of the room — the Ballast Control Process Simulator and the rest of the Simulator Suite are built for hands-on interaction, and watching someone else click through them is a much weaker version of the exercise.

  4. 4

    Pair the accordion sub-sections with a "read, then explain back" pattern. Every framework and reference page on this site uses the same expand/collapse sub-card structure — asking a learner to summarize one sub-card aloud is a quick, low-stakes comprehension check.

  5. 5

    Reinforce the Safeguards policy before the first hands-on lab, not just once at the start of the course — a two-minute reminder before Lab 1 keeps the isolated-network and defensive-scope rules front of mind.

07

Adapting for Self-Paced Delivery

Every lesson, lab, and simulator page in this course is a self-contained, browser-based artifact — nothing requires a live instructor to function. That makes the course adaptable to self-paced delivery with a few adjustments.

Replace live discussion with written reflection

Have self-paced learners write a short response to one Section 04 discussion prompt per strand instead of a live group discussion, submitted alongside their lab reports.

Front-load the prerequisite check

Put the Section 02 diagnostic at the very start of the self-paced track so a learner can self-assess readiness before committing to the full 12 lessons.

Set a pacing target, not a hard schedule

Suggest the same Day 1–5 grouping as a five-session target (e.g., one evening per "day") rather than a fixed calendar, so the strand logic still holds.

Build in an asynchronous check-in

A short async office-hours channel or forum, checked by an instructor a few times a week, replaces the live "ask a question mid-lesson" option self-paced learners otherwise lose.

📖 Teaching Note

The capstone (Lesson 12) is the hardest lesson to run fully self-paced, since it's designed around peer critique. For a self-paced cohort, substitute a written peer-review exchange between two learners, or have the instructor provide the "peer" review themselves using the same rubric described in the Assessment & Rubric Bank.
