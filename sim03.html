An alarm monitoring system (AMS) log is one of the richest sources of evidence available after an incident — but only if someone actually reads it with a critical eye. This simulator pairs with Lesson 11, where alarm monitoring coverage gaps and log correlation are covered as core investigative skills.

Below is a fixed, scrollable slice of a real-looking AMS log spanning about 25 minutes on a single watch. Read through it, then answer the questions beneath it about what you notice.

Q1.One entry in this log is timestamped earlier than the entry immediately before it, even though it appears later in the scrolling order. What does that represent?
Q2.Between the 02:21:09 badge scan and the 02:31:57 alarm, the AMS-CORE heartbeat — which otherwise appears roughly every 1–3 minutes — is missing entirely. What does the 02:31:57 entry represent?
Q3.Which log line represents a device/access event occurring somewhere it plausibly shouldn't, worth flagging on its own even without any alarm severity attached?

What this teaches

This is the practical shape of Lesson 11's alarm monitoring coverage-gap discussion: an AMS is only as trustworthy as its continuity, and a missing heartbeat is itself a signal, not an absence of one. It also previews why accurate time matters so much for this work — an out-of-order entry is only visible because every device is assumed to be reporting against the same clock. Lesson 11 covers GNSS-derived time synchronization as the quiet dependency underneath every correlation review like this one; when that shared clock can't be trusted, neither can the order of events you're looking at.
