The Maritime OT Threat Landscape

GPS and AIS spoofing/jamming, ransomware crossing from a port's business network into terminal operations, nation-state interest in shipping lanes, and crew/vendor insider risk — taught through documented incidents read as case studies.

Lesson Overview

This lesson is not a how-to. Every incident described below is drawn from public, widely reported sources — regulatory advisories, investigative journalism, post-incident company statements, and researcher reporting — and every one of them is discussed at the level of cause, impact, and lesson learned, never as a reproducible technique. That framing matters more here than in almost any other lesson in this course, because the subject matter is threats, and the line between "understanding a threat well enough to defend against it" and "understanding a threat well enough to reproduce it" is a line this course does not cross.

With that framing set, the lesson has one job: give you a working map of who is actually interested in maritime OT, and what documented interference with navigation, tracking, and terminal operations has actually looked like in the real world. We cover four threat actor categories, two well-documented classes of navigation-data manipulation (GNSS spoofing/jamming and AIS spoofing), the single most consequential publicly documented case of ransomware crossing from a business network into port operations — the 2017 NotPetya attack on Maersk — and the insider and supply-chain risk profile that is distinctive to a crewed vessel and its vendor ecosystem.

Anchoring analogy

Think of this lesson as the maritime equivalent of a hospital's infection-control briefing. Nobody teaches infection control by walking staff through how to culture a pathogen — they teach how outbreaks actually started, how they spread through shared equipment and staff movement, and what containment measures worked. That is exactly this lesson's posture toward maritime cyber incidents: how they started, how they spread across shared infrastructure, and what stopped them — never the pathogen itself.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Identify the threat actor categories relevant to maritime OT — nation-state, criminal/ransomware, hacktivist, and insider — and their maritime-specific motivations.
  • Explain GPS/GNSS spoofing and jamming, the difference between them, and their documented real-world use against shipping.
  • Explain AIS spoofing and manipulation, and why AIS should be treated as an aid to navigation rather than ground truth.
  • Describe how ransomware has crossed from IT into port and terminal OT, using the 2017 NotPetya/Maersk incident as the primary case study.
  • Describe insider and supply-chain risk specific to crewed vessels and port vendors.
Part A · Foundations Lecture

Threat actor categories in maritime OT

≈ 25 min

A threat actor is any individual or group whose actions could compromise the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of a system — or, in maritime OT terms, the safety and continuity of a vessel or port operation. Maritime OT draws interest from roughly four overlapping categories, and each one shows up in the documented incident record with a recognizably different motive.

Nation-state actors are government-affiliated or government-directed groups with interests that track geopolitics: intelligence collection on shipping movements, positioning for potential disruption of strategic shipping lanes and chokepoints during a crisis, or degrading an adversary's logistics and situational awareness. Their maritime interest is strategic rather than transactional — a nation-state actor is rarely trying to extract a ransom from a single vessel operator; it is trying to understand or influence what an entire region's shipping traffic looks like.

Criminal / ransomware actors are financially motivated. Maritime and port logistics operators make attractive targets precisely because disruption is expensive and time-sensitive — a container terminal that cannot process gate transactions loses revenue and generates cascading delays by the hour, which is exactly the kind of pressure a ransomware operator is counting on to motivate a fast payment.

Hacktivist actors are motivated by a cause rather than money or state interest — protest against a specific shipping route, a cargo type (fossil fuels, live animal transport, arms shipments), or a company's environmental or labor record. Their maritime activity tends to target visibility (a defaced tracking feed, a public AIS anomaly) more than deep, sustained OT compromise.

Insiders are people who already have legitimate access — crew, port workers, or vendor technicians — who misuse that access, whether deliberately (a disgruntled or coerced individual) or through negligence (a default password never changed, a USB drive from an unknown source plugged into a bridge PC). Because so much of maritime access control still runs on trust in a small crew or a familiar vendor technician, insiders are a persistent and often underweighted category.

Teaching note

Learners often expect a fifth category — "terrorist" — modeled on aviation or land-transport security training. Point out that in the documented maritime incident record, the categories above account for nearly everything publicly attributed, and that lumping every serious incident under "terrorism" tends to produce worse risk analysis, not better, because it obscures the very different motives and capabilities in play.

GPS/GNSS spoofing and jamming

≈ 35 min

GNSS jamming and GNSS spoofing are both forms of interference with satellite navigation, but they work by denial versus deception, and the distinction matters for how a bridge team should respond to each. Jamming overwhelms the very weak GNSS signal with noise on the same frequency, so a receiver loses its fix entirely — the equipment typically knows it has lost signal and alarms accordingly. Spoofing is more insidious: it presents a receiver with a signal that looks legitimate but carries false position or time data, and a receiver that has been successfully spoofed often has no built-in way to know its fix is wrong — it reports the false position with the same confidence it would report a true one.

Neither of these is theoretical. Multiple governments, researchers, and maritime safety authorities have published advisories over the past decade documenting sustained GNSS interference affecting commercial shipping in specific regions — most consistently reported in the Black Sea and in the Persian Gulf / Strait of Hormuz, where the U.S. Maritime Administration (MARAD) and the U.S. Coast Guard Navigation Center have both issued vessel-facing advisories describing observed interference. Publicly reported effects have included vessels' navigation systems and AIS-derived tracks showing positions many miles from a ship's actual location, tracks appearing to move at physically impossible speeds, and, in some widely circulated cases, ship icons on tracking maps appearing to sit on dry land or overlapping an airport runway. This lesson describes those documented, observed effects — not the technical means by which any specific interference was produced.

Anchoring analogy

Jamming is a jackhammer outside your window during a phone call — you can't hear anything, and you know it. Spoofing is someone quietly cutting into the phone line and impersonating the person you thought you were talking to, fluently enough that you keep believing them. A bridge team can usually tell when jamming has knocked out their GPS fix, because the equipment says so. A spoofed fix, by contrast, can look completely normal on the display while being entirely wrong.

Worked example

A bridge team transiting a region with documented GNSS interference notices their ECDIS-displayed position has jumped several kilometers inland in a matter of seconds, with no corresponding change in heading, speed, or engine order. Radar and visual references — coastline, other vessel traffic, a known landmark — still show the ship exactly where the crew expects it to be. The confusion this creates is exactly the point of this module: the electronic picture and physical reality have diverged, and the correct response is not to trust whichever display looks more confident, but to cross-check GNSS-derived position against radar, visual fixes, and, if needed, revert to the dead-reckoning and celestial-navigation skills covered in Lesson 1 as a backup.

Teaching note

This is a good moment to reinforce a theme that will recur through Lesson 9's governance material: regulatory guidance in this area consistently tells crews to report suspected interference and to maintain proficiency in non-GNSS position-fixing methods — not to expect a purely technical fix. Treat "GNSS can be wrong and not know it" as the single takeaway you want every learner to leave this module with.

AIS spoofing and manipulation

≈ 25 min

Lesson 5 established that AIS messages carry no built-in authentication — any transmitter broadcasting on the AIS frequencies with a plausible-looking message is accepted by receivers at face value. That design choice, made when AIS was built primarily as a collision-avoidance aid among cooperating vessels, is exactly what makes AIS data manipulation possible, and it has been documented in several forms in open-source maritime research and investigative reporting.

Documented patterns include vessels broadcasting a false Maritime Mobile Service Identity (MMSI) or identity to obscure their true origin or ownership — a pattern researchers and sanctions-monitoring organizations have associated with vessels seeking to obscure movements subject to trade restrictions; AIS tracks showing a vessel's reported position diverging sharply from independent satellite-imagery or radar confirmation of its actual location; and, in isolated but well-documented cases, transmission of fabricated distress or navigational-warning messages. Because AIS is broadcast in the open and easy to monitor, these manipulations are also comparatively easy for researchers, coast guards, and vessel-tracking services to detect and cross-reference after the fact — which is one reason so much of this pattern is publicly documented rather than merely suspected.

Worked example

A watch officer on a vessel approaching a busy strait sees an AIS target on the ECDIS reporting a vessel name, course, and speed that appear entirely ordinary. Radar, however, shows no corresponding return anywhere near that reported position, and a separate AIS target with a different identity appears to occupy the physical location where a return does exist. The correct response, consistent with how officers are already trained to use AIS, is to treat the AIS picture as one input among several — never as a substitute for radar and visual observation — and to report the anomaly through the vessel's normal safety-reporting channel.

Teaching note

Tie this directly back to Lesson 5's protocol-level point about AIS lacking authentication. Learners retain "AIS is an aid, not ground truth" far better when they can connect it to the specific technical reason (no authentication in the message format) rather than treating it as an arbitrary rule.

Ransomware crossing from IT into port operations

≈ 35 min

The single most consequential, most thoroughly publicly documented example of ransomware disrupting maritime operations is the June 2017 NotPetya attack and its impact on A.P. Moller–Maersk. NotPetya was a fast-spreading, destructive piece of malware — publicly attributed by multiple Western governments to Russian state-linked actors and widely assessed to have been aimed primarily at Ukrainian organizations via a compromised accounting-software update — that spread far beyond its apparent original target once it reached organizations with shared, unsegmented Windows domain infrastructure. Maersk, which had a Ukrainian office running the compromised software, was one of many multinational companies caught in that wider spread.

The publicly reported operational impact was severe: Maersk has stated that the malware forced the company to rebuild its core IT environment — reported at roughly four thousand servers and forty-five thousand PCs — over about ten days, and that container-terminal operations at multiple ports handled by Maersk-affiliated terminal operators (including APM Terminals facilities) were disrupted, in some cases forcing staff to fall back to manual processes for booking, gate transactions, and vessel-loading coordination while systems were rebuilt. Maersk publicly estimated the total financial impact in the hundreds of millions of dollars. Critically, NotPetya was not written to target maritime OT specifically — the disruption at the terminals happened because business-network systems (domain controllers, DNS, and the terminal operating systems that coordinate gate and crane operations) sat close enough to the compromised business network that the malware's automated spread reached them anyway.

The general pattern this case illustrates recurs across many less-publicized maritime and port incidents: ransomware rarely starts on an OT network. It starts with a foothold on the business side — a phishing email, a compromised remote-access credential, a vulnerable internet-facing service — and then spreads laterally using whatever shared infrastructure connects business systems to operational ones. When a terminal operating system, a gate-control application, or a crane scheduling system depends on the same Windows domain, the same DNS servers, or the same flat network as the business systems that were compromised first, that operational system can be knocked offline even though no attacker ever specifically targeted it.

Teaching note

Resist the temptation to let learners conclude "so OT was never really the target, therefore OT security wasn't the issue." The opposite lesson is the one this course wants to land: because OT and IT shared infrastructure with no meaningful boundary, an attack that never targeted OT still took it down. That is precisely the ungoverned-convergence problem Lesson 1 previewed and Lesson 2 built a zone model to address — and it is the direct throughline into this lesson's simulation and into Lesson 7's segmentation module.

Insider and supply-chain risk

≈ 25 min

Two risk categories are distinctive to the maritime environment in ways that don't map cleanly onto a typical shore-based IT security program: crew-related insider risk, and the vendor/shipyard supply chain.

A vessel's crew rotates on a cycle measured in weeks to a few months, not years — a very different turnover rate than a typical shore office. Every rotation is an opportunity for access to be granted, and every departure is an opportunity for access to be forgotten rather than formally revoked. Shared bridge and engine-room consoles, generic logins used across a whole watch rotation, and contractor or port-agent access granted for a single call and never cleanly closed out are all recurring findings in maritime cyber assessments. Lesson 10 will build a full identity lifecycle model — provisioning, rotation, and deprovisioning — around exactly this problem; this lesson's job is simply to name it as a distinct risk category and connect it to the insider-actor category from Module 1.

The vendor and shipyard supply chain is the second distinctive category. Bridge and engine-control equipment, ECDIS software, and satellite communication terminals are all built, configured, and periodically updated by outside vendors — sometimes remotely, sometimes during a yard period. Each of those touchpoints is a potential path for risk that has nothing to do with the vessel's own security posture: a vendor's remote-support channel with standing credentials, a firmware or software update package that carries unexpected code because the vendor's own build or distribution process was compromised, or hardware delivered with an insecure default configuration nobody on the vessel side ever reviewed. Because the vessel operator typically cannot inspect a vendor's internal development or distribution security, supply-chain risk has to be managed contractually and architecturally — through vetting, update verification, and the segmentation and remote-access controls Lesson 7 covers — rather than assumed away.

Anchoring analogy

Insider and supply-chain risk are two versions of the same underlying problem: trust extended to someone who is not, in that moment, standing where you can watch them. A crew member with forgotten bridge access is trust that was never revoked. A vendor's compromised update package is trust that was extended to a company you can't audit yourself. Neither requires a malicious actor aboard the ship at all — that is exactly why both categories are so persistently underweighted.

Key Terms
  • GNSS SpoofingBroadcasting false satellite-navigation signals so a receiver computes and reports an incorrect position or time as if it were valid.
  • AIS SpoofingBroadcasting false AIS identity, position, or status data, exploiting AIS's lack of built-in authentication.
  • RansomwareMalware that disrupts or extorts access to systems or data, which can cross from a business network into OT via shared infrastructure even without targeting OT directly.
  • Insider RiskRisk arising from people who already hold legitimate access — crew, port staff, or vendors — misusing or losing control of that access.
  • Supply Chain RiskRisk introduced through equipment, software updates, or remote-support channels provided by outside vendors and shipyards.
