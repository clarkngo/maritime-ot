Access Control & Identity Management Afloat

Roles for master, officers, engineers, and shore-based vendors; the shared bridge-console problem; privileged remote access over satellite; and identity lifecycle across a crew that rotates every few months.

📊 Intermediate ⏱ 4 hours 🎯 5 objectives
Overview Roles Aboard Shared Console Problem PAM & Jump Hosts MFA at Sea Identity Lifecycle Activity Simulation Review

Lesson Overview

A vessel's crew is small, hierarchical, and constantly changing, and its systems were mostly designed decades before anyone expected a per-user login prompt on a bridge console. Shore-side identity and access management assumes a large, relatively stable population of employees, a help desk, and a network connection that is always there. None of that holds cleanly aboard ship. This lesson works through what access control and identity management looks like when the "workforce" is a rotating crew of a few dozen people, the "network" is a hull surrounded by water with an intermittent satellite link to the outside world, and some of the most safety-critical consoles on the vessel were never built with individual user accounts at all.

We start with who actually needs access to what — master, officers, engineers, the ship's own network administrator, and the shore-based vendors who occasionally need to reach in from outside — then work through two problems that don't have clean shore-side analogues: bridge and engine consoles built around a single shared login, and multi-factor authentication that quietly stops working the moment the vessel loses satellite coverage. We close with the identity lifecycle problem every operator eventually runs into: a crew member who signs off after a nine-month contract, and an account that nobody remembered to disable.

Anchoring analogy

Think of a shore office's identity system as a hotel with a front desk that's staffed around the clock — badges are issued and revoked the same day, and IT is always one phone call away. A vessel's identity system is closer to a remote research station: a small, fixed roster of people who rotate in and out on a fixed schedule, no help desk down the hall, and stretches of time when nobody can call anyone. The access-control choices that make sense in the hotel — a login prompt for every door, a push notification for every sensitive action — don't always survive the trip to the research station unchanged, and this lesson is about knowing where they do and where they don't.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Describe role-based access needs aboard a vessel, including master, officers, engineers, the Electro-Technical Officer (ETO), and shore-based vendors.
  • Explain the "shared console" problem common on bridge and engine-room systems, and its tradeoffs for accountability.
  • Describe privileged access management (PAM) and jump-host patterns for remote OEM and vendor access over a satellite link.
  • Evaluate where multi-factor authentication (MFA) is and isn't practical on a vessel given intermittent connectivity.
  • Describe identity lifecycle management across a crew that rotates every few months.

Roles aboard: who needs access to what

≈ 30 min

A ship's crew is organized into a strict, well-understood hierarchy long before anyone thinks about network accounts, and that hierarchy is a reasonable starting point for role-based design — as long as you remember it was built to answer "who is responsible for what," not "who should have which system login." The master holds ultimate responsibility for the safety of the vessel, its crew, and its cargo, and in practice that means the master needs, at minimum, administrative visibility across bridge and safety systems and the authority to approve exceptions — including authorizing a vendor's remote session — even if the master rarely operates those systems hands-on day to day.

Deck officers (chief, second, and third officer, depending on vessel size) stand navigational watches and need operational access to ECDIS, radar/ARPA, autopilot, and the bridge alarm monitoring system — the instruments a watchkeeper actually touches. Engineering officers (chief and second engineer, and others depending on vessel class) need equivalent operational access to the engine automation system, ballast control, and the engine-room side of alarm monitoring. The Electro-Technical Officer (ETO), where the vessel carries one, is typically the only crew member with a formal network administration role — managing switches, firewalls, and user accounts across the ship's OT and IT networks — which makes the ETO's own account the single most powerful (and highest-value, from an attacker's perspective) identity aboard. Shore-based vendors and superintendents sit outside the crew roster entirely: their need for access is real but intermittent, tied to a specific piece of equipment and a specific support ticket, not to standing operational responsibility.

Teaching note

Learners with an IT background will want to jump straight to "just build an RBAC matrix." Let them — then ask what happens when the third officer who normally covers the 8-to-12 watch is reassigned mid-voyage to cover for an engineer who went home sick. Shipboard roles shift under operational pressure in ways a static shore-side org chart rarely has to.

The shared console problem

≈ 30 min

Walk onto most bridges and into most engine control rooms and you'll find the same pattern: the ECDIS, the alarm monitoring system, the engine automation HMI are logged in under a single shared operator account, often left logged in for the entire voyage. This is not an oversight so much as a deliberate accommodation of how watchkeeping actually works. A watch changes hands every four hours, sometimes at short notice, often in the middle of a maneuver, a pilotage, or a weather event — and a login prompt demanding a username and password at exactly that moment is not a minor inconvenience, it is an operationally unacceptable delay on a system where a few seconds of inattention during a course change can matter.

The tradeoff this creates is squarely an accountability and audit-trail problem: if the ECDIS shows a manual route deviation at 03:40, the system log alone cannot tell an investigator which of three officers who stood watch that night made it — the login record just says "Bridge," the same account it has said for the entire voyage. Full per-user login enforcement is the obvious fix on paper and the wrong answer operationally for most bridge and engine HMIs as they exist today. Partial mitigations exist instead: physical key switches that restrict who can even reach certain functions, and — the most commonly relied-upon mitigation in practice — cross-referencing the electronic system log against the paper or electronic deck log and watch schedule, which records who was actually on watch at a given time even though the system itself doesn't.

Worked example

A bulk carrier's ECDIS has been logged in under the shared "Bridge" account since departure four days earlier. An investigator reviewing a near-miss with a fishing vessel needs to know who was conning the ship at 03:40. The ECDIS log shows only "Bridge — route deviation, 03:40:12." Cross-referencing the deck log and the watch schedule narrows it to the third officer, who stood the 00:00–04:00 watch that night — a conclusion the system log alone could never have supported.

Anchoring analogy

A shared bridge login is closer to a shift logbook at a 24-hour control room than to an individual employee badge. Nobody expects a power-plant control room operator to log out and a replacement to log back in mid-shift during an abnormal event — the handover happens by briefing and by signing the shift log, not by a screen prompt. The accountability comes from the paper trail running alongside the system, not from the system itself.

Privileged access and jump hosts for remote vendor support

≈ 35 min

Lesson 1 described remote vendor support as one of the four business pressures that eroded a vessel's historical isolation, and Lesson 7 covered it again as a countermeasure design problem. This module is where those two threads meet the identity question directly: when an OEM technician ashore needs to log into the autopilot or the engine automation system to diagnose a fault, what should that session actually look like?

The pattern most maritime OT security programs converge on is a jump host — a single, tightly controlled intermediary system that mediates every remote session rather than allowing the vendor to connect directly to the target equipment. A well-designed jump-host arrangement for shipboard use gives every remote session four properties: it is authenticated with an individually attributable credential rather than a shared one; it is logged, ideally with session recording, so there is a complete record of exactly what commands were run; it is time-boxed, opened for a specific support window rather than left standing; and — critically, for a vessel — the crew retains the physical or administrative ability to disconnect or disable the session immediately if anything looks wrong, without waiting on shore-side approval. This last property directly satisfies the intent of IACS UR E26's remote access requirement, covered in Lesson 8, which expects exactly this kind of crew-controlled, logged, and time-limited remote-access capability rather than an always-open vendor connection.

Together, this cluster of controls is what privileged access management (PAM) refers to: managing the smaller set of accounts and sessions that carry elevated or administrative capability — vendor remote sessions, the ETO's own administrative account, any shore-side account with write access to OT systems — with tighter controls than ordinary operational accounts get, because a compromise of one of these accounts is disproportionately damaging.

Worked example

An autopilot vendor requests remote access to investigate an intermittent fault. The ETO opens a two-hour session on the vessel's jump host, issues the technician a single-use credential scoped only to the autopilot's diagnostic interface, and the session is recorded. Twenty minutes in, the officer of the watch notices unexpected diagnostic traffic on the bridge network switch and terminates the jump-host session from the local console — without needing to contact anyone ashore first. The vendor calls back to request a new session once the officer is satisfied nothing unsafe is occurring.

Teaching note

If your learners covered Lesson 8's UR E26 material recently, this is a good moment to have them locate the specific remote-access clause from memory and match it against the four jump-host properties listed above — it reinforces that the regulation and the practical control are the same idea described twice.

Where MFA works — and where it doesn't — at sea

≈ 30 min

Multi-factor authentication (MFA) is close to a solved problem for shore-side systems: a fleet-management portal, a crewing platform, or a shore office email account can rely on a push notification or a one-time SMS code because the person authenticating almost always has a reliable data or cellular connection at the moment they need it. Aboard a vessel mid-ocean, that assumption breaks. A push-notification MFA app needs an internet connection to receive the push; an SMS-based code needs cellular service, which a ship in open water simply does not have. When the vessel is out of VSAT coverage — during a satellite handover, a weather-related outage, or a deliberately bandwidth-conserving mode — push- and SMS-based MFA can fail completely, at exactly the moment someone aboard needs to authenticate to handle whatever caused the connectivity issue in the first place.

The practical alternatives are methods that don't depend on a live connection at the moment of authentication: hardware tokens generating time-based one-time passwords (TOTP) work entirely offline once provisioned, as do offline-capable authenticator apps that generate codes locally on the device rather than receiving them by push. For shore-side portals with reliable connectivity, standard push- or app-based MFA remains appropriate and should be required. For certain shipboard systems — particularly ones where the alternative to password-only access is no access at all during an outage, and where the system is already isolated from the internet by network segmentation — password-only access, kept strong and unique per account, can be a documented, risk-accepted compromise rather than a security failure, provided that acceptance is a deliberate decision recorded in the vessel's cyber risk assessment rather than a default nobody examined.

Teaching note

Push back gently on any learner who proposes "just require MFA everywhere aboard" as a complete answer. The point of this module isn't that MFA is a bad idea at sea — it's that the specific mechanism matters, and a control that silently fails during exactly the kind of connectivity disruption that might accompany an incident is worse than a documented, deliberate exception.

Identity lifecycle across a rotating crew

≈ 35 min

Most shore-side identity programs manage employee turnover measured in years. A vessel manages turnover measured in months: contracts typically run somewhere between three and nine months depending on rank, flag state, and operator policy, which means a mid-sized crew of 20–25 can generate well over a hundred sign-on and sign-off events in a single year across the whole crew roster. Every one of those events is, in identity-management terms, a provisioning or deprovisioning action, and the identity lifecycle is the discipline of making sure each one actually happens, on time, in both directions.

Onboarding — provisioning access before or at the moment a crew member boards — is usually the easier half to get right, because there's a clear operational trigger (a new officer needs to stand a watch, so their ECDIS and alarm-monitoring credentials need to exist) and a clear moment it needs to happen by. Offboarding is the harder half. Revoking a departing crew member's access promptly at sign-off requires someone — usually the ETO, sometimes the master — to actually execute the deprovisioning step at the moment of departure, and that step competes for attention with everything else happening during a crew change in port. When connectivity is limited, deprovisioning that depends on syncing with a shore-based identity system can lag by days or weeks even when the local action was taken promptly, and it can be skipped entirely when it wasn't.

The accumulated result, across a vessel's operating life, is a familiar and largely preventable risk: stale accounts. An account created for a third officer's nine-month contract in 2022 that was never explicitly disabled doesn't disappear on its own — it sits there, often with a weak or long-unchanged password, on a system nobody is actively watching for exactly this. Over several years and several rounds of crew turnover, a vessel that never established a clean deprovisioning process can accumulate a meaningful number of accounts belonging to people who haven't been aboard in years — each one a credential an attacker (or a disgruntled former crew member with a memorized password) doesn't have to work very hard to find.

Worked example

A second engineer completes a seven-month contract and signs off in Rotterdam. The ETO who normally handles account deprovisioning is mid-handover to a relief ETO and the departure happens during a busy two-day port call; the departing engineer's engine automation system account is never disabled. Eight months later, during a routine access review ahead of an ISM audit, the account turns up still active — unused since the sign-off date, but valid, with the same password the engineer set at the start of the contract.

Teaching note

This is a natural bridge to Lesson 9's governance material: identity lifecycle failures are exactly the kind of finding a periodic access review (a governance control) is designed to catch, precisely because they are so easy to miss operationally in the moment.

Key Terms
  • RBACRole-based access control — granting system access according to a person's shipboard role (master, officer, engineer, ETO, vendor) rather than to each individual separately.
  • PAMPrivileged Access Management — the tighter set of controls applied to accounts and sessions carrying elevated capability, such as vendor remote access and network administration.
  • Jump HostA single controlled intermediary system that mediates remote sessions, providing logging, time-boxing, and crew-controlled disconnection.
  • MFAMulti-factor authentication — requiring more than one form of proof of identity; some methods depend on live connectivity that a vessel may not always have.
  • Identity LifecycleThe end-to-end process of provisioning access when a crew member joins and revoking it when they leave, across a crew that rotates every few months.
