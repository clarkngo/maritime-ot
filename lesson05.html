Maritime & Industrial Communication Protocols

NMEA 0183 and NMEA 2000, AIS message formats, Modbus and CAN bus in the engine room, and the terminal-side protocols that move a container from ship to yard — framing at a level you can read in a capture.

Lesson Overview

Lesson 4 covered the general-purpose IT protocols — Ethernet, TCP/IP, VLANs, DHCP, DNS, NTP — that carry traffic around a modern vessel and port terminal network. This lesson goes one layer up, into the maritime- and industrial-specific protocols riding on top of that network: the sentence and message formats that actually tell an ECDIS where the ship is, that broadcast a vessel's identity and position to everyone nearby, and that let a crane's programmable logic controller know when to lower its spreader onto a container.

None of these protocols require you to memorize bit-level frame layouts to reason about their security posture. What matters for this course is a working sense of what each protocol carries, how it is structured well enough to recognize in a packet capture, and — the thread that ties the whole lesson together — what each one does, or does not, do to verify that a message came from where it claims to have come from. NMEA 0183, NMEA 2000, and AIS all inherit the "trust the wire" assumption from Lesson 1; Modbus carries the same assumption into the engine room and the port terminal; and OPC UA is the one protocol in this lesson that was actually designed with authentication and encryption as optional, built-in features.

Anchoring analogy

Reading these protocols is a bit like learning to read shorthand road signs from a country you've never visited. You don't need to memorize the entire traffic code — you need to recognize the shape of a stop sign versus a yield sign versus a speed limit sign well enough to know what kind of message you're looking at, and, for this course, whether that sign could have been put up by literally anyone with a can of paint.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Describe the structure of an NMEA 0183 sentence, including talker/formatter codes and the checksum field.
  • Describe how NMEA 2000 operates over a CAN bus and how Parameter Group Numbers (PGNs) address different data types.
  • Describe the message types AIS transponders broadcast and the kind of data each one carries.
  • Describe how Modbus, PROFINET, and OPC UA are used in port terminal and engine-room automation.
  • Explain the authentication — or lack of it — built into each protocol covered in this lesson.

NMEA 0183: sentence structure and how it's read

≈ 35 min

An NMEA 0183 sentence is a single line of plain ASCII text, always starting with a dollar sign, and organized as comma-separated fields ending in a checksum. The first field after the dollar sign is a five-character talker/formatter code: the first two characters identify the type of device sending the sentence — GP for a GPS receiver, for instance — and the remaining three identify the sentence type itself, describing what kind of data follows. A sentence beginning $GPGGA is therefore a GPS fix data sentence from a GPS talker. The sentence ends with an asterisk, a two-character hexadecimal checksum computed by XOR-ing every character between the dollar sign and the asterisk, and a carriage return/line feed. The checksum catches transmission errors — a corrupted bit somewhere in the sentence — but, notably, it verifies nothing about who sent the message; it only confirms the message wasn't garbled in transit.

Worked example — decoding a GGA sentence

Consider the sentence $GPGGA,123519,4807.038,N,01131.000,E,1,08,0.9,545.4,M,46.9,M,,*47. Reading it field by field: GPGGA is a GPS fix data sentence; 123519 is the UTC time of the fix, 12:35:19; 4807.038,N is latitude 48°07.038′ North; 01131.000,E is longitude 011°31.000′ East; 1 is the fix quality indicator (1 meaning a standard GPS fix); 08 is the number of satellites used; 0.9 is horizontal dilution of precision; 545.4,M is altitude above mean sea level in meters; 46.9,M is the geoid separation; the two empty fields are for differential GPS data not in use here; and *47 is the checksum. A learner who can walk through those fields in order has everything needed to recognize this sentence type in a live capture, without needing to memorize the NMEA specification.

Teaching note

If time allows, put a real (or realistic sample) NMEA 0183 capture on screen and have learners identify the talker code, sentence type, and checksum before you walk through the fields together. The pattern-matching skill — recognizing structure at a glance — matters more here than memorizing every sentence type.

NMEA 2000 and CAN bus

≈ 30 min

NMEA 0183, as Lesson 1 covered, is fundamentally a point-to-point protocol: one talker, one dedicated wire, one (or a small handful of) listeners per circuit. NMEA 2000 was built to remove that limitation by riding on top of a CAN (Controller Area Network) bus — the same bus technology widely used in automotive and industrial control — which lets many devices share a single network trunk instead of each needing its own dedicated wire. A single NMEA 2000 backbone can carry position data, engine parameters, and wind and depth sensor readings from a dozen or more devices all sharing the same cable run.

Where an NMEA 0183 sentence identifies itself by a talker/formatter code, an NMEA 2000 message identifies itself by a PGN (Parameter Group Number) — a numeric identifier that says what kind of data the message contains (vessel heading, engine RPM, GPS position, and so on). Think of a PGN as the CAN-bus equivalent of the sentence-type portion of an NMEA 0183 talker code: it tells every device on the bus what kind of data just went by, so each device can pick out only the PGNs it cares about and ignore the rest. Like the CAN bus it rides on, NMEA 2000 has no built-in sender authentication — any device physically wired onto the bus can transmit any PGN it chooses, and every other device on the bus will accept it as legitimate.

AIS: what it broadcasts and how

≈ 35 min

AIS (Automatic Identification System) transponders — Class A, required on most SOLAS commercial vessels since the 2002 carriage mandate covered in Lesson 1, and the lower-power Class B used by smaller and recreational craft — continuously broadcast a vessel's identity and navigational state over VHF radio to any receiver within range: other ships, shore stations, and satellites. A typical AIS position report carries the vessel's MMSI (Maritime Mobile Service Identity, a unique numeric identifier), current position, SOG/COG (speed over ground and course over ground), heading, navigational status (under way using engine, at anchor, restricted in ability to maneuver, and so on), and rate of turn. Separate, less frequent messages carry static and voyage-related data: vessel name, call sign, ship type, dimensions, destination, and estimated time of arrival.

On the wire, AIS messages are encapsulated inside NMEA sentences using the AIVDM/AIVDO format — !AIVDM for a message received from another vessel, !AIVDO for a message about your own vessel's transmission — carrying a binary-encoded payload rather than the comma-separated plain text of a GGA sentence. You do not need to decode that binary payload bit by bit for this course; what matters is recognizing the AIVDM/AIVDO wrapper and understanding, at a conceptual level, which fields are inside it. What matters even more for this course is what AIS does not do: there is no cryptographic signature, no sender verification, and no built-in mechanism to confirm that a received AIS message actually originated from the vessel whose MMSI it claims. Any radio transmitter capable of the correct signaling can broadcast a technically well-formed AIS message claiming to be any vessel, at any position, doing anything — a gap Lesson 6 explores directly through AIS spoofing.

Worked example — reading an AIS position report conceptually

A watch officer's AIS display shows a target report: MMSI 366123456, vessel name "COASTAL TRADER," position 47.61°N / 122.33°W, SOG 12.3 knots, COG 047°, navigational status "under way using engine." Conceptually, that report tells the officer who the vessel claims to be (MMSI and name), where it claims to be (position), how fast and in what direction it claims to be moving (SOG/COG), and what it claims to be doing (navigational status) — four separate claims, none of them independently verified by the AIS system itself. The officer's radar, by contrast, reports only what is physically detected reflecting radar energy back — it makes no claims about identity at all. Module 5's simulation in this lesson explores what happens when those two pictures disagree.

Anchoring analogy

AIS is closer to a boat shouting its own name and heading across open water than to a passport check at a border crossing — everyone nearby can hear the announcement, and it's usually accurate, but nothing stops someone else from shouting a false name just as loudly, and there's no gate agent checking papers to catch it.

Modbus and CAN bus in the engine room

≈ 35 min

Modbus, in both its serial (Modbus RTU) and networked (Modbus TCP) forms, remains one of the most common protocols in engine automation and auxiliary machinery monitoring, decades after its original release in 1979 for industrial programmable controllers ashore. Its longevity comes from the same source as its security weakness: it is a simple, openly documented, request-response protocol with a small set of function codes — a controller (the "master") sends a request such as "read holding registers starting at address X," and a device (the "slave") replies with the requested data. That simplicity made it trivial for equipment vendors across many industries, marine included, to implement decades ago, and trivial for it to keep working with minimal changes ever since.

The tradeoff is that Modbus, in its common legacy form, has no built-in authentication whatsoever. Any device that can reach a Modbus slave over the network (or the serial bus) can send it a legitimate-looking read or write request, and the slave has no protocol-level way to confirm the request came from the authorized engine control system rather than from something else that found its way onto the same network segment. Alongside Modbus, plain CAN bus (independent of the NMEA 2000 profile covered in Module 2) is also widely used directly in machinery control — generator control panels, engine electronic control units, and auxiliary equipment often communicate over a CAN bus using vendor-specific message formats rather than the standardized NMEA 2000 PGN scheme, but with the same lack of built-in sender authentication.

Teaching note

Learners with an IT security background will recognize Modbus's "no authentication" story as extremely similar to what they may already know about Modbus in land-based industrial control systems — because it is the same protocol, largely unchanged. This is a good moment to reinforce that maritime OT security draws heavily on decades of land-based ICS security research; the protocols and their weaknesses did not originate at sea.

Port terminal protocols: Modbus TCP, PROFINET, OPC UA

≈ 30 min

Moving a container off a ship and onto a chassis in the yard is, underneath the cranes and the choreography, an industrial automation problem — and it runs on the same general-purpose industrial protocols found in any heavy-manufacturing facility ashore, rather than on anything maritime-specific. A ship-to-shore crane's PLC (programmable logic controller) commonly communicates with sensors, drives, and safety systems over Modbus TCP or PROFINET — a Siemens-originated, now widely adopted, real-time industrial Ethernet protocol popular in crane and conveyor automation for its deterministic timing. Both inherit the strengths and weaknesses already discussed: efficient, well-understood, and — like Modbus in the engine room — generally without built-in authentication in their common configurations.

OPC UA (Open Platform Communications Unified Architecture) is the protocol most likely to sit above these device-level networks in a modern terminal, serving as the interoperability layer that lets a terminal operating system (TOS) — the software that assigns cranes, tracks container moves, and schedules yard equipment — talk to OT devices from many different vendors without a custom integration for each one. What sets OPC UA apart from every other protocol in this lesson is that it was designed from the outset with optional, built-in security features: message signing and encryption, and certificate-based authentication between client and server, all specified as part of the standard rather than bolted on afterward. Those features are optional, not automatic — an OPC UA deployment configured without them offers no better authentication guarantee than plain Modbus — but the capability exists in the protocol itself, which is more than can be said for NMEA 0183, NMEA 2000, AIS, or legacy Modbus.
