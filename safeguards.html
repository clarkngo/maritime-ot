01

Physical & Operational Safety

Maritime OT controls physical processes — propulsion, steering, ballast, cargo, cranes. A mistake made against a live system, or against bench hardware handled carelessly, can injure a person, damage equipment, or in the worst case contribute to a vessel casualty. Safety governs every other rule in this policy, including the ones about scope and data below.

If a hands-on activity ever creates a real tension between "finish the exercise as designed" and "do the safe thing," the safe thing wins, every time, without needing permission. This applies whether the risk is to a person, to bench hardware, or to a network the lab shares with anything else. No lesson objective, deadline, or demonstration is worth an injury or an equipment casualty.

Any activity that involves physical equipment — bench PLCs, actuator mockups, wiring, powered hardware of any kind — requires:

  • Lockout/tagout (LOTO) on any circuit or actuator being serviced, disconnected, or bypassed, verified by the supervising instructor before hands begin.
  • Appropriate PPE for the task — eye protection at minimum around any powered mechanism, with additional PPE as the specific equipment requires.
  • A qualified supervisor physically present for the entire activity, not just at setup — this is not a rule that can be satisfied by a checklist signed in advance.

A learner or instructor who is unsure whether an activity is safe to continue stops and asks before proceeding, rather than assuming the exercise design accounted for the situation. Section 08 covers how to raise a concern if something about a planned exercise looks unsafe before the lab even starts.

02

Lab Environment Requirements

Every hands-on activity in this course runs against simulated systems, virtual PLCs, or bench hardware acquired specifically for teaching — never against an operating vessel, a live ECDIS, or a working port terminal. The environment itself is a safeguard, and it has to be verified, not assumed.

Labs use the course's browser-based simulators (see the Simulator Suite) and, where physical hardware is used, bench equipment owned and maintained for this course alone. Nothing in this curriculum is designed to be pointed at a production vessel network, a commissioned port terminal system, or any equipment currently in operational service.

Any lab network used for protocol-level exercises (packet capture, Modbus/NMEA traffic generation, and similar) stays physically or logically air-gapped from any production ship or shore network, and from the general internet where the exercise doesn't specifically require it. Isolation is verified before every session — a segment that was isolated last week is not assumed to still be isolated today.

Verification checklist before every hands-on session

  1. 1

    Confirm the lab segment's isolation — no uplink to a production network, and no unexpected bridge back to the campus or corporate LAN.

  2. 2

    Confirm equipment identity — the PLC, sensor, or bench rig in front of the learner is course-owned lab hardware, not a device on loan from an operating system.

  3. 3

    Confirm supervision — a qualified instructor or lab assistant is present for any activity involving physical equipment (Section 01).

  4. 4

    Confirm the exercise scope — the planned activity matches what Section 03 defines as in-scope, with no live exploitation or attack tooling involved.

03

Defensive Scope & Case-Study Boundaries

This course teaches how to understand, harden, monitor, and respond to maritime OT systems — not how to attack them. That boundary is deliberate and applies to every lesson, lab, and generated artifact.

⚠ No live exploitation, no attack tooling, no working exploit code

In scope

Architecture and segmentation, protocol literacy, hardening and compensating controls, detection and monitoring, incident response process, governance and framework literacy.

Out of scope

Working exploit code, live malware samples, attack tooling aimed at navigation, propulsion, or cargo/terminal control systems, and any instruction framed as "how to compromise" rather than "how to defend."

Vulnerability discussion

Named CVEs and documented weaknesses (Lesson 7) are discussed at the level needed to understand and prioritize a defense — not at the level of a working proof-of-concept.

Framework analysis

Standards work (IACS UR E26/E27, NIST SP 800-82, IEC 62443, and the rest of the Core Frameworks set) is read and applied, never bypassed or "worked around" as an exercise.

Documented real-world events — GPS and AIS spoofing incidents, port ransomware, and other maritime cyber incidents referenced in Lesson 6 and Lesson 11 — are analyzed for root cause, impact, and lessons learned. They are taught as case studies to reason about, never reconstructed step-by-step as a procedure a learner could run against a live target. Every lesson card that touches incident material carries an explicit "Case study analysis only" flag.

04

Data Handling

Real vessel and port operator information is sensitive by nature — network diagrams, class-society survey detail, and terminal configuration can be commercially sensitive or safety-relevant even outside a security context.

Any vessel network diagram, class-society survey finding, port operator configuration, or other commercially sensitive material a learner or instructor encounters outside this course stays out of shared coursework — quizzes, discussion posts, published lab reports, and generated content alike.

Where a real-world example is genuinely useful for teaching, it is scrubbed of identifying detail (vessel name, IMO number, operator, specific IP addressing) or replaced entirely with a synthetic equivalent before it is used in a lesson, lab, or assessment. Every simulator and lab exercise in this course (see Simulator Suite) uses entirely synthetic data for exactly this reason.

The same rule applies to learners who work in or around the maritime industry: capstone projects, lab reports, and discussion contributions (see Assessment & Rubric Bank) should draw on synthesized or anonymized detail rather than an employer's actual configuration or incident history.

05

AI-Generated Content Review

Portions of this course's lesson text, quiz items, graphics, and supporting material are produced with the help of generative tools (see the Instruction Prompt Building Tools on the course hub). Generated output is treated as a first draft, never as a finished, trusted artifact.

Any AI-generated lesson text, quiz question, diagram, or activity description gets a subject-matter accuracy review and a source check before it is used with learners or shared further — whether it came from the course's own generation tools or elsewhere. Technical claims about maritime systems, protocols, and incidents are verified against the frameworks and sources this course already cites (see each framework page's own Sources section) rather than taken on faith.

Using a generation tool does not transfer responsibility for correctness away from the person who publishes the result. Whoever puts generated material in front of learners is accountable for having reviewed it, the same as any other teaching material they author.

06

Accessibility & Equity

A safeguards policy that only protects some learners isn't doing its job. This course is built to be usable without a paid license, a specific device, or particular sensory or motor abilities.

WCAG 2.2 AA target

Course pages, including this one, aim to meet WCAG 2.2 Level AA — sufficient color contrast, visible focus states, keyboard operability, and semantic structure for assistive technology.

No paid-license dependency

No lab or simulator in this course requires a commercial vendor license, paid software seat, or account a learner cannot freely obtain. The Simulator Suite runs entirely in-browser for this reason.

Keyboard operability

Every interactive element — accordions, theme toggle, table of contents, simulators — is operable from a keyboard alone, not just with a mouse or touchscreen.

Reduced-motion support

Pages respect a learner's operating-system preference for reduced motion, disabling non-essential transitions rather than forcing them.

07

Learner Acknowledgment

Before a learner's first hands-on lab, they read this policy in full and provide the acknowledgment below — in writing, or through whatever mechanism the delivering institution or instructor uses to record it (a signed form, an LMS quiz gate, or a verbal confirmation logged by the instructor for a small in-person cohort).

Sample Acknowledgment Statement

"I have read and understood the Safeguards & Responsible Use Policy for Maritime Operational Technology Security. I understand that all lab activities are simulation-based, that I will not attempt to interact with any live vessel, port, or production system as part of this course, and that I will handle any real-world data I encounter with appropriate care."

  1. 1

    When it's required: before the learner's first hands-on lab (see Hands-on Lab Exercises), not merely before the course begins.

  2. 2

    Who collects it: the instructor or lab supervisor of record, who keeps a record appropriate to their delivery context.

  3. 3

    What it unlocks: access to hands-on labs and physical-equipment sessions specifically — reading lesson content does not require it, but hands-on participation does.

08

Reporting a Concern

Any learner or instructor who believes a planned or in-progress exercise crosses one of these boundaries — physical safety, environment isolation, defensive scope, or data handling — should raise it before proceeding, not after.

  1. 1

    Stop the activity if it's already underway and the concern is safety-related (Section 01's "when in doubt, stop" rule applies immediately, without waiting for a response).

  2. 2

    Raise it with the instructor or lab supervisor directly and describe specifically what about the exercise seems out of bounds — which section of this policy it seems to touch is a useful starting point.

  3. 3

    Expect the exercise to be paused or modified until the concern is resolved, rather than proceeding on the assumption it will turn out to be fine.

  4. 4

    Escalate beyond the instructor through the delivering institution's normal channel (department chair, training coordinator, or equivalent) if the concern involves the instructor's own conduct or goes unaddressed.
