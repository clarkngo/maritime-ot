Most shipboard systems don't have their own reliable clock — they get time the same way they get position: from GNSS (the Global Navigation Satellite System family, of which GPS is one member), typically distributed ship-wide over an onboard NTP (Network Time Protocol) server. Lesson 4 introduces this GNSS-fed NTP source as the quiet dependency behind almost every timestamp on a vessel. Lesson 6 covers real-world GNSS spoofing case studies. Lesson 11 depends on all of it: log and alarm correlation only works if every device's timestamp can be trusted against the same clock.

This simulator makes that dependency visible. Toggle spoofing on and watch the ship's clock silently diverge from true UTC — and read what that divergence would mean for every system downstream of it.

What this teaches

This cross-references three lessons deliberately. Lesson 4 establishes that shipboard NTP is only as trustworthy as the GNSS signal feeding it — there is no independent shipboard time source most vessels fall back on by default. Lesson 6 covers documented GNSS spoofing incidents where vessels received false position and, less visibly, false time. Lesson 11 is where the cost becomes concrete: once the AMS, VDR, and network logs no longer agree with true UTC — or, worse, no longer agree with each other — a correlation review built on "what happened, and in what order" loses its foundation. A false position on ECDIS is the visible symptom; a silently accepted false time is the deeper one, because it undermines every record the ship keeps about everything else.
