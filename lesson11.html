Monitoring, Detection & Maritime OT Incident Response

Passive asset discovery on a vessel LAN, alarm-log coverage gaps, GNSS-derived time synchronization (and what spoofing does to it), fleet-wide SOC triage over constrained bandwidth, and incident response when you cannot simply unplug a ship mid-voyage.

Lesson Overview

Everything in this course so far has been about reducing the odds that something goes wrong on a vessel's OT network — segmentation, governed convergence, access control, framework compliance. This lesson is about what happens once you accept that, despite all of that work, something eventually will. Monitoring and detection aboard ship has to answer a narrower, harder question than its shore-side counterpart: how do you build visibility into a network you cannot safely probe the way an IT team probes an office LAN, using log sources that were never designed with an investigator in mind, over a link too thin to just ship everything home, on a platform you usually cannot simply take offline the moment something looks wrong?

We start with how a vessel builds an asset inventory (the one Lesson 9 told you to build) without ever sending a single unsolicited packet at a live piece of bridge or engine equipment. From there we look at where the alarm monitoring system — the closest thing most vessels have to a security event log — quietly stops telling you anything useful, and at how GNSS spoofing, introduced in Lesson 6, does its real damage not to navigation but to an investigator's ability to line up events across systems after the fact. We close with the two constraints that make maritime incident response its own discipline rather than a copy of shore-side IT incident response: a shore-based fleet security operations center that has to prioritize blind, over a satellite link, which of dozens of vessels actually needs attention right now; and a master who may not be able to isolate an affected system without creating a safety problem worse than the cyber one.

Anchoring analogy

A shore-side security operations center is like a hospital's emergency department: patients (alerts) arrive continuously, staff triage them in person, and a doctor can order any test needed on the spot. A fleet SOC watching forty vessels over VSAT is more like a telemedicine service covering forty remote clinics that can each only send a short, expensive text message describing symptoms — the SOC has to decide, from that text message alone, which clinic gets a call back first, and which one gets asked to run the expensive full diagnostic panel and mail in the results. Good triage in that world isn't about reacting to everything; it's about knowing which thin signal is worth escalating.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain why passive asset discovery, not active scanning, is the preferred way to build and maintain an asset inventory on a vessel OT network.
  • Describe common alarm-monitoring-system log coverage gaps on field devices, and the investigative blind spots they create.
  • Explain how GNSS spoofing undermines time-based log correlation across onboard systems, building on Lessons 4 and 6.
  • Describe how a shore-based fleet SOC triages alerts across many vessels over constrained satellite bandwidth.
  • Describe incident response constraints unique to a vessel that cannot simply be isolated or shut down mid-voyage.
Part A · Foundations Lecture
🔍

Why passive asset discovery, not active scanning

≈ 30 min

Lesson 9 told you to build an asset inventory — device, zone, criticality, vendor, firmware version, last service date — as the foundation everything else in a maritime OT security program stands on. It did not say how to build one on a live vessel network, and that turns out to matter a great deal. In shore-side IT, the default way to discover what's on a network is active scanning: a tool like an network mapper sends probe packets to every address in a range and infers what's there from the responses. On an OT network, and especially a shipboard one, that same technique is a well-documented way to break things.

Many marine electronics and industrial controllers — legacy PLCs, older sensor gateways, single-purpose alarm points — run minimal, resource-constrained TCP/IP stacks that were built to handle exactly the traffic their one talking partner sends them, and nothing else. A scan that floods a switch segment with unexpected connection attempts, malformed packets, or simply an unfamiliar volume of traffic can cause one of these devices to hang, reboot, or drop out of the control loop it was quietly running — on a network where "the device that just crashed" might be reading a tank level or feeding the alarm monitoring system. Passive monitoring avoids this entirely: instead of sending anything, it listens. A network tap or a switch's span (mirror) port copies existing traffic to a monitoring device, which builds its picture of the network — what devices exist, what protocols they speak, who talks to whom, how often — purely by observing normal operation. Nothing is injected onto the wire; a device that has never once been probed can still be fully inventoried, as long as it talks to something else during the observation window.

Worked example

A security assessor preparing a vessel's Lesson 9-style asset inventory considers running a quick active scan of the engine control network to save time. The ETO flags that the network still includes a fifteen-year-old temperature sensor gateway that has never been patched and was not designed to handle unsolicited connection attempts. The assessor instead connects a laptop to a span port configured on the switch, captures forty-eight hours of normal operating traffic, and builds the inventory from what the gateway and its neighbors actually say to each other during routine operation — arriving at the same device list without sending it a single packet it wasn't already expecting.

Teaching note

Learners with an IT security background will sometimes push back that modern vulnerability scanners have safe modes and rate limiting. That's a fair point to acknowledge — but the underlying lesson is about default posture: on a network you cannot fully characterize in advance, and where equipment age is unusually wide, passive discovery is the safer starting assumption, not a fallback for when active scanning "isn't available."

Alarm monitoring system log coverage gaps

≈ 30 min

The alarm monitoring system (AMS) is the closest thing most vessels have to a centralized security event log, and it is tempting to treat it as one. It isn't — it was designed to tell a watchkeeper that a defined condition has crossed a threshold (high engine temperature, low tank level, a system fault), not to give an investigator a complete record of what led up to that condition. Two coverage gaps show up on nearly every vessel an investigator examines after the fact.

First, many field devices simply don't report to the AMS at all. Older sensors and simple PLCs wired for local control may never have been integrated into the ship-wide alarm bus, either because they predate it or because nobody saw a reason to connect a device that "just does one thing" to a centralized system. Second, and more subtly, even devices that do report to the AMS frequently log only the alarm state itself — "high temperature alarm active" — without the underlying telemetry that would explain how the reading got there. The AMS tells you a threshold was crossed; it usually cannot tell you the trend leading up to that moment, because it was never asked to store readings that didn't cross a threshold.

Common alarm monitoring system coverage patterns
Device typeTypical AMS reporting behaviorInvestigative gap it creates
Modern integrated bridge/engine HMIReports both alarm states and underlying values continuouslyMinimal — usually the best-instrumented part of the ship
Networked sensor with alarm threshold logicReports alarm state only when threshold is crossedNo visibility into the trend before the alarm fired
Legacy PLC or simple sensor gatewayNot integrated into the AMS bus at allNo record in the AMS of the device's behavior, alarmed or not
Third-party OEM subsystem (e.g. a standalone reefer or cargo monitoring unit)Logs locally to its own proprietary system, not the AMSData exists but sits outside the record an investigator would normally check first
Worked example

An investigator reconstructing an engine trip pulls the AMS log and finds a single entry: "Main engine lube oil pressure — low alarm, 09:14:52." The AMS was never configured to store the pressure trend leading up to that alarm, and the sensor feeding it is not integrated with the ship's separate condition-monitoring platform, which does retain trend data but was not the investigator's first stop. Two extra days pass — and two extra requests to the vessel — before the investigator learns the trend data exists at all, in a system nobody thought to mention because it isn't called an "alarm" system.

Teaching note

This is a good moment to have learners generate their own list of "systems on a ship that keep some kind of log" beyond the AMS — condition monitoring platforms, OEM diagnostic tools, network switch logs, the VDR. The point isn't that the AMS is useless; it's that treating it as the single source of truth is exactly the assumption that costs investigators time.

GNSS time sync and what spoofing does to correlation

≈ 35 min

Lesson 4 made the case for why vessels commonly use a GNSS receiver as the master time source for shipboard NTP: it's accurate, it's already being received continuously for navigation, and it doesn't depend on the VSAT link being up. That module flagged, and deferred, one consequence — if the GNSS signal itself can be manipulated, the ship's sense of time is exposed to the same manipulation as its sense of position. Lesson 6 then covered GNSS spoofing directly: a receiver fed a plausible-looking but false signal typically has no way to know its fix — of position or time — is wrong, and reports it with full confidence.

For an investigator, that has a specific and serious consequence: log correlation. Reconstructing an incident timeline means lining up entries from the AMS, the VDR, and network traffic captures against a shared clock, so that "alarm fired," "helm order given," and "unusual network traffic observed" can be placed in the correct sequence relative to one another. That only works if every source's timestamps trace back to a trustworthy shared reference. If the GNSS-derived NTP source was spoofed or degraded during the window under investigation — even briefly — some or all of those timestamps may be systematically offset, and nothing about the individual logs will announce that fact. Two systems that actually recorded events six seconds apart can appear to show them happening in the wrong order, or three minutes apart, and an investigator working from the logs alone has no built-in way to detect the distortion.

Anchoring analogy

Investigating an incident without a trustworthy shared clock is like trying to reconstruct a car accident from three witnesses' accounts when each witness's watch is silently running at a different, unknown speed. Each witness can still tell you what they saw — but "it happened right before the horn" from one witness and "it happened right after the horn" from another might both be true statements from watches that were never actually synchronized, and you have no way to tell which witness to trust without an independent reference clock to check them against.

Worked example

During a suspected GNSS interference event in a region flagged by MARAD advisories, a vessel's shipboard NTP server — sourced from GNSS, per Lesson 4's default configuration — briefly accepted a spoofed time signal along with the spoofed position data. Two hours later, investigators reviewing an unrelated network anomaly pull the AMS log, the VDR data, and a network capture, expecting all three to share a common timeline. The AMS entries, timestamped from the (temporarily) corrupted NTP source, are offset by several minutes relative to the VDR, which draws its own timestamp from a separate onboard clock. The investigation stalls until someone thinks to check whether the vessel transited a known GNSS interference area during the window in question — the same interference that had already been logged, and shrugged off, as a navigation issue.

Teaching note

Tie this explicitly back for learners: Lesson 4 introduced GNSS-sourced NTP as a sensible design choice, and Lesson 6 introduced GNSS spoofing as a documented navigation threat. This module is where those two facts combine into a security consequence neither lesson stated on its own — an independent, non-GNSS time reference (even a simple onboard clock with its own drift-monitoring) is a meaningful resilience measure, not just a navigation backup.

Fleet SOC triage over constrained bandwidth

≈ 30 min

Lesson 4 established that a vessel's total VSAT bandwidth is a scarce, often metered resource shared across every system and every crew member aboard. A shore-based fleet security operations center (fleet SOC) watching a fleet of dozens of vessels has to build its entire monitoring model around that constraint, because the obvious shore-side answer — pull continuous full packet captures and verbose logs from every vessel, all the time — is neither affordable nor, in most cases, technically possible over the link available.

The pattern most fleet SOCs converge on instead is tiered: each vessel periodically sends a compact heartbeat or summary report — a small message covering system health indicators, alarm counts by category, and basic connectivity status, typically every few hours — rather than raw logs. The SOC's analysts watch for patterns across the fleet in these summaries: a vessel whose alarm count spikes relative to its own baseline, a vessel that misses a scheduled heartbeat entirely, several vessels in the same region reporting similar anomalies at the same time. Only when a summary crosses a threshold that warrants a closer look does the SOC escalate to requesting a targeted data pull from that specific vessel — a bounded set of logs or a short packet capture covering a defined window, sized and scoped deliberately because every megabyte pulled over VSAT has a bandwidth and cost impact the vessel's other systems and crew will also feel.

Fleet SOC reporting tiers and when each is used
Reporting patternTypical frequency / sizeWhen the SOC relies on it
Heartbeat / summary reportEvery few hours; a few kilobytesContinuous baseline monitoring across the whole fleet
Targeted log pullOn demand; scoped to a system and time windowA summary anomaly warrants a closer look at one vessel
Full packet capture requestRare; can be large and slow over VSATA confirmed or suspected incident requires forensic-quality evidence
Worked example

A fleet SOC covering 35 vessels notices, from routine heartbeat reports, that one bulk carrier's alarm-count summary has run roughly 40% above its own six-month baseline for two consecutive reporting cycles, with no corresponding weather or maintenance event logged. That pattern alone doesn't justify pulling a full packet capture — the cost and bandwidth impact would be disproportionate to what's known so far. Instead, the SOC requests a scoped, twelve-hour AMS log export from that one vessel, reviews it ashore, and only escalates to a full capture request once the export shows a pattern consistent with a genuine anomaly rather than a sensor recalibration the crew had simply not logged.

Incident response when you can't just unplug the ship

≈ 35 min

A typical shore-side IT incident response playbook treats isolation as an early, almost reflexive step: disconnect the compromised host from the network, contain the blast radius, investigate from a position of safety. Aboard a vessel mid-voyage, that reflex can be exactly the wrong first move. A navigation or propulsion-adjacent system suspected of compromise may also, at that exact moment, be the system keeping the vessel safely on its intended course through a traffic separation scheme, a strait, or a stretch of heavy weather. Isolating it without a plan for what replaces its function is not a purely cybersecurity decision anymore — it's a decision that can create a safety hazard more immediate than the incident it was meant to contain.

This is why maritime incident response puts a specific person in a specific role that shore-side IT playbooks don't have an equivalent for: the master's authority to weigh safety against security containment and decide, in the moment, which risk is larger. A risk-based containment decision on a vessel might mean continuing to operate a suspect system under close manual supervision until the ship reaches safe water, open sea with sea room to maneuver, or a scheduled port call — deliberately delaying full isolation rather than accepting an immediate safety risk to address a security one. That is not a failure to follow procedure; on a vessel, it frequently is the procedure, and it is the master, not a shore-based security team, who has the authority and the responsibility to make that call.

Anchoring analogy

Isolating a shore office's compromised file server is like turning off a room's lights to stop a fire from an electrical fault — inconvenient, but nobody in the building depends on that particular light staying on to avoid walking into a wall. Isolating a vessel's steering or engine control system mid-transit is closer to turning off a patient's ventilator to investigate a fault in it — the fix has to account for what keeps the patient alive while you work, not just for stopping the fault. A vessel's containment plan has to answer the "what keeps us safe in the meantime" question before it answers the "how do we cut this off" question.

Worked example

The bridge crew observes unexplained diagnostic traffic from the autopilot's network interface while transiting a narrow, heavily trafficked channel. Fully isolating the autopilot would force the watch officer onto manual steering during the most demanding stretch of the transit — a moment of genuinely elevated collision risk. The master decides to keep the autopilot in service, with an officer stationed to manually override at the first sign of erratic behavior, until the vessel clears the channel into open water roughly ninety minutes later — at which point the autopilot is isolated, the jump-host access log from Lesson 10 is pulled, and the ETO begins recovery using the vessel's UR E26-mandated backup and recovery procedure, covered in Lesson 8, rather than waiting for a shore IT team that cannot physically reach the ship.

Teaching note

Connect this directly to Lesson 8's IACS UR E26 material: UR E26 requires a documented, tested means of recovering critical systems after a cyber incident precisely because a ship mid-voyage cannot wait for outside help to arrive. Post-incident recovery planning is not a separate topic from the containment discussion in this module — a vessel's containment posture and its recovery capability have to be designed together, because a decision to delay isolation only makes sense if there is a tested plan for what recovery looks like once it's safe to act.
