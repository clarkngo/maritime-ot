IT/OT Security Principles at Sea

Why maritime OT inverts the CIA triad, why safety of life at sea sits above everything else, and a zone model for reasoning about vessel and port networks.

📊 Intermediate ⏱ 4 hours 🎯 5 objectives

Lesson Overview

Lesson 1 traced how a vessel's network stopped being isolated from shore. Before we can talk about defending that network, we need a vocabulary for what "defending" even means at sea — and it turns out that vocabulary is not the one most security professionals learn first. Nearly every introductory security course opens with the CIA triad — Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability, usually in that order — because it was written for information systems whose worst-case failure is a data breach. A ship underway has a worse failure mode than a data breach, and that single fact reorders everything.

This lesson builds the four ideas that everything else in the course rests on: the maritime priority stack that puts Safety, Availability, Integrity, and Confidentiality in that order rather than IT's usual confidentiality-first arrangement; the international legal backbone — SOLAS and the ISM Code — that makes safety non-negotiable rather than merely preferred; the operational reality of determinism and continuous availability on a vessel that cannot simply be taken offline mid-voyage; the mismatch between a three-to-five-year IT refresh cycle and a twenty-to-thirty-year vessel service life; and a five-zone reference model for classifying any piece of equipment you encounter aboard a ship or in a port facility, from a rudder angle sensor to a shore-based fleet dashboard.

Anchoring analogy

A corporate email server's worst day is a leaked inbox. A ship underway is closer to a hospital intensive care unit than to an email server: the heart monitor that is imperfectly calibrated but running beats the heart monitor that has been switched off so a technician can patch it with a clean conscience. Security instincts built around the office — encrypt everything, lock down every login, block unrecognized traffic by default — all have to be re-checked against the possibility that, this time, the "server" is keeping a ballast alarm alive.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain how the maritime OT priority order (Safety, Availability, Integrity, Confidentiality) differs from the standard IT CIA triad.
  • Describe SOLAS and the ISM Code's role as the umbrella safety obligation that maritime OT security decisions must respect.
  • Explain why determinism and continuous availability matter more on a vessel mid-voyage than in a typical office IT environment.
  • Contrast typical 3-5 year IT refresh cycles with 20-30 year vessel/equipment lifecycles and what that means for patching and vendor support.
  • Apply a vessel/port zone reference model, analogous to the Purdue model, to classify a piece of equipment by zone.

The inverted triad at sea

≈ 30 min

Every introductory security course teaches the CIA triad in roughly the same order, because in a typical office IT environment the worst-case failure is a data breach: a leaked customer database, a stolen credential, a proprietary file exfiltrated to a competitor. Confidentiality dominates that world, because information systems exist primarily to store and move information, and the primary harm from a compromise is that the wrong person reads or copies it.

A ship's OT priority order runs in the opposite direction, with one addition above the triad entirely. On a vessel underway, the first question a security decision has to answer is not "could this leak information?" but "could this hurt someone, or endanger the ship?" That gives four tiers rather than three, ranked in this order: Safety, Availability, Integrity, Confidentiality — sometimes written as SAIC to distinguish it from the office world's CIA. Safety sits above the triad because a maritime OT security decision that improves confidentiality or integrity at the cost of safety of life or safety of navigation is not a security improvement at all — it is a new hazard wearing a security badge.

Availability outranks integrity at sea because a system that is running and imperfect is usually safer than a system that is perfectly consistent but offline. An ECDIS with a slightly stale chart correction is still giving the watch officer a picture to work from; an ECDIS taken down to guarantee data integrity is giving them nothing. Confidentiality still matters — nobody wants a vessel's cargo manifest, crewing details, or ship security plan exposed — but it is the tier maritime OT security is willing to sacrifice first when a control's costs and benefits genuinely conflict, and IT security is usually not built to make that trade.

Anchoring analogy

Think of the standard CIA triad as a bank's priority order and the maritime stack as a hospital's. A bank protects the ledger above all else — confidentiality and integrity of the numbers are the whole business. A hospital protects the patient above the chart: a heart monitor running on slightly outdated firmware and still beeping beats a perfectly patched monitor that has been powered down for the update. Maritime OT security borrows the hospital's instinct, not the bank's.

SOLAS, the ISM Code, and why safety outranks the exercise

≈ 30 min

The maritime industry's safety-first order is not a cultural preference — it is backed by international law. The International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea (SOLAS), first adopted in the wake of the Titanic disaster and amended repeatedly since, is the treaty that sets the baseline safety requirements for the vast majority of the world's commercial ships: construction standards, life-saving equipment, fire protection, navigation equipment carriage, and — since amendments introduced in the 1990s and 2000s — elements of onboard safety management. SOLAS is, in effect, the umbrella that every other maritime safety and security obligation sits under, including maritime OT security.

Sitting underneath SOLAS is the International Safety Management (ISM) Code, which requires every SOLAS-covered operator to run a documented Safety Management System (SMS): company-level policies, shipboard procedures, and a chain of accountability running from the vessel's master up to a shore-based Designated Person Ashore, all aimed at making sure safety and pollution-prevention decisions actually get made — and actually get followed. A maritime OT security program does not sit outside the SMS; it has to be written into it. A change to a bridge or engine-control network is, functionally, a change to the safety management system, whether or not the person making the change thinks of it that way.

The practical consequence for security work is blunt: a control that could ever compromise safety of navigation or safety of life is a non-starter, no matter how sound it is as pure IT security practice. "Technically correct" and "acceptable aboard a SOLAS vessel" are not the same test, and every proposed control has to clear both.

Worked example

A shore-based IT security team, tasked with hardening a newly converged vessel network, proposed a default-deny firewall rule between the engine control LAN and the alarm monitoring segment, intending to reduce lateral-movement risk after a penetration test flagged the two segments as too permissive. Tested in a lab, the rule performed exactly as designed — until a vessel engineer pointed out that the same segment carried the ballast pump high-level alarm feed to the bridge. Under the proposed rule, a ballast tank overfill during cargo operations would trigger no bridge alarm at all. The rule was technically sound IT security practice and an unacceptable safety regression at the same time; it was rewritten, with a narrower scope carved out for the alarm feed, before it went anywhere near a real ship.

Teaching note

This is exactly the kind of outcome Lesson 1's discussion of ungoverned convergence previewed: a reasonable-sounding IT control, proposed without a maritime safety review, quietly threatens the SOLAS-level obligation the network exists to protect in the first place. A functioning maritime OT security program routes every control change that touches a safety-related signal path through the vessel's SMS change process — not around it.

Determinism and availability mid-voyage

≈ 35 min

A deterministic system produces the same output, within a predictable and bounded time, for the same input — every time. An autopilot's control loop, a steering gear's response, a ballast valve's actuation all depend on this property: the operator, or another automated system reading the result, needs to know not just that the command will eventually execute, but that it will execute within a guaranteed window, every time, without some background process introducing unpredictable delay.

A typical office IT environment can tolerate the opposite. A file server that pauses for two seconds to run a virus scan, an application server that reboots automatically at 2 a.m. to apply a patch — mildly annoying, quickly forgotten. A vessel four hundred miles from the nearest port does not get that grace period. Its ECDIS, its alarm monitoring system, its steering control cannot simply "take a moment" or "come back after a quick restart" whenever it happens to suit a shore-side patch calendar — because there may be a watch officer relying on that exact system, at that exact moment, to avoid a hazard that IT scheduling has no visibility into at all.

This is what "you can't just take it down" means operationally: not that OT systems are never patched or rebooted, but that every maintenance action mid-voyage has to be scheduled around the vessel's actual operational state — open ocean versus restricted waters, steady transit versus a scheduled course change, routine passage versus cargo operations — rather than around a shore IT team's convenience.

Worked example

During a routine software update window, a vessel's alarm monitoring system (AMS) was rebooted by a shore-scheduled patch job while the ship was making a planned course change through a traffic separation scheme. For the ninety seconds the AMS took to restart, the bridge team lost consolidated visibility into engine, fire, and bilge alarms — instruments that would normally have surfaced a developing problem before a human noticed it manually. No incident occurred, but the incident report that followed flagged the update as scheduled without regard for the vessel's operational state, and the fleet's patch policy was rewritten to require watch-officer sign-off before any bridge-system reboot, regardless of how routine the patch.

Teaching note

Determinism and availability mid-voyage are exactly why frameworks written for enterprise IT — including most default hardening guides — need maritime-specific interpretation before they are applied to a bridge network. Lesson 8 works through how frameworks such as NIST SP 800-82 and IEC 62443 get adapted, rather than applied verbatim, to vessel OT.

20-30 year hull lifecycles vs. 3-5 year IT refresh

≈ 30 min

A typical enterprise IT refresh cycle runs three to five years: laptops, servers, and even most business applications are budgeted to be replaced or substantially upgraded on that cadence, in part because the security model assumes it — vendors stop patching old operating systems on a predictable schedule, and the organization simply buys new hardware rather than defend an aging one indefinitely.

A vessel does not work that way. Ships are commissioned for twenty-five to thirty-year service lives, and a large share of the OT equipment installed at newbuild — control PLCs, HMI panels, the underlying computers running ECDIS or the alarm monitoring system — remains original-fit for fifteen years or more before anyone seriously considers replacing it, because swapping out embedded control equipment mid-service-life means a shipyard period, class society survey, and a level of cost and logistics an IT refresh never has to clear. It is not unusual to find a bridge HMI still running an operating system whose vendor stopped issuing security patches years earlier, simply because the equipment still does its job and there is no economically sane way to replace it at sea.

Comparing typical IT refresh assumptions with shipboard OT equipment lifecycles
AspectTypical office ITVessel / shipboard OT equipment
Refresh interval3-5 years, budgeted and routine15+ years for embedded control equipment; hull service life of 20-30 years
OS support windowVendor mainstream support typically 5-10 yearsLegacy or end-of-life Windows versions often still running in production
Patch cadenceMonthly or continuous, remotely pushedOpportunistic — tied to yard periods, port calls, and operational windows
Replacement cost & logisticsSwap a laptop or server the same dayRequires a shipyard period, class survey, and vendor engineering support
Anchoring analogy

Patching an office laptop is like changing a smoke detector battery — quick, cheap, and done without a second thought. Replacing a ballast control PLC is closer to replacing a building's structural steel — technically possible, but it needs a plan, a permit, a specialist crew, and a window when the building isn't occupied. Security programs that assume every asset can be patched like a laptop will keep proposing plans a ship simply cannot execute.

A zone model for vessel and port networks

≈ 40 min

Industrial control security borrows heavily from the Purdue Enterprise Reference Architecture, a layered model that separates field-level sensors from control systems, from supervisory HMIs, from the business network, from the wider enterprise and internet. Maritime OT security teaches a maritime-specific adaptation of the same idea: a five-zone reference model that gives a vessel or port network a common vocabulary for "how far is this device from the open internet, and how much would it hurt if it failed."

Anchoring analogy

If you already know the Purdue model from industrial security, map Zone 0-2 loosely onto Purdue Levels 0-2 and Zone 3-4 onto Levels 3-5. The maritime version simply renames the layers around a vessel's actual equipment — a rudder angle sensor instead of a factory flow meter, a bridge HMI instead of a plant control room — rather than a factory floor's process units.

The five zones, from the physical process outward to the open internet:

The maritime vessel/port zone reference model
ZoneExample equipmentTypical criticality / trust level
Zone 0 — Field devices & sensorsRudder angle sensor, tank level sensorHighest safety relevance; simplest devices, usually no independent security controls of their own
Zone 1 — Control systemsAutopilot, ballast control PLC, engine governorVery high; closed-loop control acting directly on Zone 0 data — availability and determinism are paramount
Zone 2 — Bridge/engine HMI & monitoringECDIS, alarm monitoring system, conning displayHigh; the human supervisory layer watching and directing Zones 0-1
Zone 3 — Shipboard business LANCrew Wi-Fi, cargo documentation systemsModerate; administrative traffic, but a common bridge to Zone 4
Zone 4 — Shore/enterprise via VSATFleet ops center, charterer systems, internetLowest trust; the vessel's only routine path to the open internet

The model is a teaching scaffold, not a wiring diagram — a real vessel's network rarely respects these boundaries as cleanly as the table suggests, which is exactly why the model is useful. Every time you cannot cleanly say which zone a piece of equipment belongs to, you have found a segmentation gap worth investigating.

Worked example

A port terminal's crane control PLCs (Zone 1) were found, during a security assessment, to be reachable directly from the terminal's guest Wi-Fi network (Zone 3-4 territory) because an integrator had left a diagnostic laptop's network bridge in place after commissioning. Classifying the PLCs by zone first — before touching a single firewall rule — made the gap obvious: a Zone 1 device should never be directly reachable from a Zone 3/4 network without at least one enforced boundary in between.

Key Terms
  • SOLASInternational Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea — the primary international treaty on ship safety, which no maritime OT security control may compromise.
  • ISM CodeInternational Safety Management Code — requires every SOLAS-covered operator to run a documented Safety Management System covering security-relevant changes.
  • DeterminismA system's ability to produce the same output within a guaranteed, predictable time — critical for control loops like autopilot and steering gear.
  • Zone ModelA maritime-specific, Purdue-model-inspired reference architecture (Zones 0-4) for classifying equipment by criticality and trust boundary.
  • AvailabilityIn maritime OT, the requirement that navigation and control systems keep running continuously — especially mid-voyage, when they cannot simply be taken offline.
