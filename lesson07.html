Lesson Overview

Lesson 6 established who is interested in maritime OT and what documented interference has looked like. This lesson turns to the other half of the risk equation: why maritime OT systems remain vulnerable in the first place, and — the part this lesson spends more time on — what actually reduces that risk when a vessel cannot simply be treated like a shore-based data center. Nothing in this lesson involves probing, scanning, or exploiting any real or simulated system. Every vulnerability class discussed here is described at the level of "what kind of weakness this is and why it persists," never as a walkthrough of how to find or abuse a specific instance of it.

The throughline is a simple but important idea: most maritime OT vulnerabilities are not the result of anyone doing something obviously wrong. They are the predictable outcome of operating equipment on a 15-to-25-year hull lifecycle, in an environment where a "quick patch" can mean re-certifying a safety-critical system, and where the next opportunity to safely touch some of that equipment might be years away. Countermeasures that assume monthly patch cycles and instantly reconfigurable networks don't survive contact with that reality — so this lesson focuses on the ones that do: segmentation, allowlisting, tightly controlled remote access, tested backups, and the broader concept of a compensating control.

Anchoring analogy

A shore-based IT system with a known vulnerability is like a car with a recalled part — you drive it to a shop this week and it's fixed. A vessel's ECDIS or engine automation system with a known vulnerability is more like a recalled part on a research station in Antarctica during the off-season: the part everyone agrees needs replacing simply cannot be reached until the next scheduled resupply, so in the meantime you manage the risk with whatever compensating measures are actually available where the equipment sits.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Identify common maritime OT vulnerability classes: legacy firmware, flat networks, default credentials, and unpatched systems.
  • Explain why a vessel's patch window is bounded by dry-dock scheduling rather than a monthly IT patch cycle.
  • Describe segmentation as a primary countermeasure, tying it to Lesson 2's zone model and the IACS UR E26 framework previewed in Lesson 8.
  • Describe allowlisting, jump-host-mediated remote access, and other compensating controls used in maritime OT.
  • Evaluate a vulnerability scenario and recommend an appropriate compensating control.
Part A · Foundations Lecture
🗄️

Legacy firmware and end-of-support systems

≈ 30 min

A vessel is typically designed for a service life of fifteen to twenty-five years, and the bridge and engine-control systems installed at newbuild are often the same firmware and operating-system versions still running when the ship is scrapped decades later. It is not unusual for a maritime cyber assessment to find embedded controllers running firmware that has not been updated since delivery, or workstation-class equipment — an ECDIS or engine-monitoring display, for instance — still running a Windows version whose mainstream vendor support ended years earlier. This is a widely acknowledged, publicly documented class of finding across maritime cyber-risk assessments and classification-society guidance, not a hypothetical.

Part of the reason this persists is technical inertia — nobody visits a bridge system as often as an office laptop — but part of it is structural. Bridge, navigation, and engine-control equipment is type-approved: a specific hardware and software configuration is tested and certified against IMO performance standards, and that certification is tied to the exact configuration tested. Changing the software — even installing a routine security patch — can, in the manufacturer's own risk calculus, invalidate that certification until it is re-verified. Because re-certification is costly and slow, some vendors have historically discouraged or even contractually restricted patching outside of a formal, vendor-managed update process, which in practice can mean long stretches with no patching at all.

Worked example

A bulk carrier's engine automation system was delivered in 2009 running an embedded operating system whose vendor ended mainstream support in 2014. The equipment still functions exactly as designed and remains within its type-approval certification. When the ship's technical manager raises the unsupported OS during a routine cyber-risk assessment, the equipment vendor's guidance is that any operating-system-level change would require re-certification the vendor is not currently prepared to fund or schedule — leaving the operator to manage the risk through network-level and procedural controls rather than a direct fix to the system itself.

Teaching note

Learners with a pure IT background often react to this module with "so patch it." Use the type-approval point to redirect that instinct: in maritime OT, "just patch it" can mean "recertify a piece of safety equipment," which is a materially bigger decision than a Windows update on an office laptop. This reframing is what makes Module 3's dry-dock patch window and Module 5's compensating controls land as reasonable rather than as excuses.

Flat shipboard networks and default credentials

≈ 30 min

Lesson 1 described how convenience-driven convergence pulled a vessel's OT network onto the same infrastructure as shore connectivity and crew Wi-Fi. One of the most consistent, publicly documented downstream effects of that convergence is a flat network — a single, unsegmented network in which navigation systems, engine-control systems, business systems, and sometimes crew devices can all reach one another, because no one ever deliberately drew boundaries between them as the network grew.

A second, closely related and equally well-documented finding is the continued presence of default or shared credentials on bridge and engine systems — a username and password left at the factory default, or a single shared login used across an entire watch rotation because provisioning individual accounts was never built into the equipment's normal operating procedure. Multiple maritime cyber-assessment firms and classification societies have published summary findings describing default credentials as one of the most commonly observed weaknesses across vessels surveyed, independent of vessel age or type. This lesson states that as a documented category of finding — not as a demonstration of how to locate or use any specific default credential.

Teaching note

Ask learners why a flat network and default credentials are being taught together rather than separately. The connection is worth drawing out explicitly: on a flat network, a single default-credentialed device isn't an isolated weak point — it's a foothold that can reach everything else on the same network, exactly the pattern Lesson 6's NotPetya/Maersk case study illustrated at the port level.

The dry-dock-length patch window

≈ 35 min

A typical IT patch cycle assumes systems can be updated monthly, or even continuously, with a reboot as the main operational cost. Maritime OT frequently cannot make that assumption. Many bridge and engine-control systems can only be safely updated during a scheduled dry-docking — a period, often every two and a half to five years depending on vessel type and class requirements, when the ship is out of service and accessible for exactly this kind of work — or during a tightly controlled maintenance window at a port call, when the relevant system can be safely taken offline without endangering navigation, cargo operations, or the safety of the crew. Outside of those windows, taking a steering-control or engine-monitoring system offline to apply an update is often simply not an option the vessel can responsibly exercise.

The practical consequence is a patch window measured in years rather than weeks. A known vulnerability identified today in a piece of navigation or engine-control equipment may remain formally unpatched not because anyone is negligent, but because the vessel's next dry-dock is genuinely eighteen months, or three years, away, and no earlier opportunity exists to touch that system without operational or safety consequences that outweigh the risk of leaving it as-is in the meantime.

Worked example

A publicly disclosed vulnerability affecting a specific ECDIS software version is confirmed to be present on a container ship's bridge system. The vessel's next scheduled dry-docking is eighteen months away, and the vendor's patch requires a supervised on-site installation that cannot be performed safely at sea or during a routine port call. Rather than leaving the system unmanaged for eighteen months, the technical superintendent applies network-level compensating controls — restricting the ECDIS's network reachability and increasing monitoring at its zone boundary — and documents the accepted residual risk and planned remediation date in the vessel's risk register, consistent with the governance approach Lesson 9 covers in depth.

Teaching note

This is the moment to name the concept the whole lesson has been building toward: a compensating control. Don't wait for Module 5 to introduce the term informally — flag it here as "the answer to what you do while you wait for the dry-dock," so Module 5 reads as a deeper treatment of something learners have already met, not a brand-new idea.

🧱

Segmentation as the primary countermeasure

≈ 35 min

If a flat network is the vulnerability that lets one weak point become everyone's problem, segmentation is the countermeasure that limits how far any single compromise can spread. Lesson 2 introduced a vessel/port zone model as a teaching scaffold — grouping systems by function and criticality (navigation, propulsion and machinery control, cargo and ballast control, general/administrative including crew Wi-Fi) and drawing a defined, verifiable boundary around each group. Lesson 8 will introduce IACS UR E26, the classification-society requirement that makes exactly this kind of security-zone architecture a mandatory design element for newbuild vessels contracted from mid-2024 onward — this lesson is where that requirement's practical logic gets built, before Lesson 8 supplies its formal vocabulary.

Three elements make segmentation work in practice. Zone boundary protection — firewalling, access control, and monitoring applied at the specific point where one zone connects to another or to a network outside the ship — is what actually enforces the boundary a zone diagram only describes on paper. A jump host is a single, tightly controlled and logged system that mediates remote access into a sensitive zone, so a vendor's remote-support session never connects directly to the engine-control network — it connects to the jump host, which is the only system inside the boundary the outside world can reach at all, and every session through it can be logged, time-limited, and reviewed (a control this lesson previews and Lesson 10 covers in depth as part of remote vendor access governance). And monitoring at zone boundaries — even simple traffic logging, rather than sophisticated intrusion detection — turns an undetected lateral-movement attempt into a visible, alertable event.

Anchoring analogy

A flat network is a cruise ship with no interior doors — a fire that starts in the galley reaches every deck. Segmentation is the fire-rated bulkheads and doors that ships are already required to have for exactly this reason: they don't prevent every fire, but they buy time and contain the damage to the compartment where it started. Zone boundary protection is those doors actually being closed and monitored, not just installed and forgotten.

Teaching note

The fire-door analogy tends to land especially well with maritime learners because it maps onto SOLAS structural fire protection requirements they may already know from safety training — reinforce that segmentation is applying a safety concept they already trust to a network design problem, not importing something foreign from IT.

Allowlisting, backups, and compensating controls

≈ 30 min

Application allowlisting — configuring a system to run only a pre-approved, known set of software and refuse anything else — is an unusually strong fit for fixed-function maritime OT precisely because these systems are fixed-function: an ECDIS or an engine-automation HMI runs a small, well-known set of software and is never expected to run anything new. That narrowness, which makes the system inflexible for general-purpose computing, is exactly what makes allowlisting practical to deploy and maintain there in a way it often isn't on a general-purpose office laptop.

Regular, tested backup and recovery procedures are the second pillar, and they matter for a reason beyond convenience: IACS UR E26 explicitly requires ship-level backup and recovery capability as part of its cyber-resilience design requirements, precisely because recovering quickly from a disruptive incident is treated as being just as important as trying to prevent one in the first place. A backup that has never been tested for restoration is a documentation exercise, not a countermeasure — the practice that actually reduces risk is periodically proving that a system can be rebuilt from the backup within an operationally acceptable time.

Both of these sit inside a broader idea worth naming explicitly: a compensating control is a control adopted specifically because the textbook fix — patch the vulnerability, replace the legacy system — isn't operationally possible right now. Network isolation around an unpatched ECDIS, credential rotation and jump-host-mediated access in place of eliminating a shared login outright, and a tested recovery plan in place of a system that simply cannot be hardened further are all compensating controls: not what the ideal security posture would look like on a blank sheet of paper, but what actually reduces risk given the constraints a vessel operator is really working within.
