01

Foundations

NIST SP 800-82 is a comprehensive guide to securing operational technology, published by the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology. Revision 3, released in 2023, deliberately widened the guide's scope beyond classic industrial control systems (ICS) — PLCs, SCADA, DCS — to cover OT broadly, including building automation and transportation systems. That breadth is what distinguishes it from the other frameworks in this course's Core Frameworks set, most of which are written specifically for ships. SP 800-82 gives a maritime learner a general-purpose OT risk-management vocabulary that applies just as well to a port crane control system or a terminal's automated gate as it does to a factory floor.

Earlier editions of SP 800-82 focused tightly on traditional industrial plant environments. By the early 2020s, "OT" had visibly expanded well past the factory floor — into building management systems, transportation networks, and port and terminal automation. The Rev. 3 rewrite responded to that expansion directly, restructuring the guide around a risk management process and a set of OT-tailored control overlays that any OT owner, not just a manufacturing plant, could apply.

OT asset owners, risk managers, and auditors across any sector — including maritime and port operators who need a defensible, standards-based way to structure a risk assessment for shore-side control systems that no ship-specific or vessel-focused framework directly covers.

SP 800-82 is the only one of the four frameworks in this set that is entirely general-purpose — it is not written for ships and it does not catalog attacker behavior. Instead, it supplies structure and control baselines that the other frameworks build on or reference: IACS UR E26/E27 is the ship-specific technical design requirement, IMO MSC-FAL.1/MSC.428(98) is the governance wrapper, and MITRE ATT&CK for ICS is the threat-intelligence layer. SP 800-82 is the risk-management scaffolding underneath all three.

04

Core Structure

SP 800-82 Rev. 3 is organized around three major structural pieces: a risk management process borrowed from and adapted to OT, a set of "overlays" that tailor the general-purpose SP 800-53 control catalog for OT constraints, and explicit alignment with the NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) so an organization's OT program can be reported in the same language as its enterprise IT program.

ProcessFoundational
Risk Management Process
A structured sequence — categorize the system, select and tailor controls, implement, assess, authorize, and monitor — adapted from the broader NIST Risk Management Framework but reframed around OT priorities like safety and availability.
ControlsBaseline
OT Overlays of SP 800-53
Tailored subsets of the general-purpose SP 800-53 control catalog, adjusted so that controls written for enterprise IT don't unintentionally disrupt an OT system's safety or availability requirements.
DesignReference
Architecture & Network Segmentation Guidance
Reference architectures and segmentation patterns — the OT-side counterpart to the zones-and-conduits thinking covered elsewhere in this course, used here as a general baseline rather than a ship-specific requirement.

Going Deeper

SP 800-82 walks an OT owner through categorizing the system by potential impact, selecting an appropriate control overlay, implementing and documenting those controls, assessing whether they work as intended, formally authorizing the system to operate, and then continuously monitoring it — a cycle rather than a one-time checklist, which matters for a port terminal's control systems that are rarely, if ever, fully "off."

An overlay is a tailored subset of the SP 800-53 control catalog, adjusted for OT constraints — most importantly, that availability and physical safety usually outrank confidentiality in OT, the reverse of the typical IT priority order. A control that assumes you can freely reboot a server to patch it, for example, gets rewritten in the overlay to account for a system that cannot be taken offline mid-voyage or mid-berth without real-world consequences.

SP 800-82 explicitly maps its OT guidance onto the five NIST CSF functions — Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover — so an OT security program can be described in the same vocabulary an organization already uses for its enterprise IT risk reporting. That shared vocabulary is often the practical reason a port authority's board or a shipping company's compliance office can understand an OT risk register at all.

05

Definitions & Scope

These terms recur throughout SP 800-82 — learners who are shaky on "overlay" in particular will misread most of Section 04 above.

OT (Operational Technology)

Hardware and software that detects or causes a change through direct monitoring and/or control of physical devices, processes, and events — the Rev. 3 scope, deliberately broader than "ICS."

Overlay

A tailored subset of SP 800-53 controls, adjusted for OT-specific constraints such as availability and physical safety, rather than applied unmodified from an enterprise IT baseline.

NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF)

The five-function (Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover) structure SP 800-82 explicitly aligns to, so OT risk can be reported in a vocabulary shared with enterprise IT.

Risk Management Process

The categorize–select–implement–assess–authorize–monitor cycle SP 800-82 adapts from the broader NIST Risk Management Framework for OT use.

Availability-First Control Tailoring

The design principle that OT controls must be adjusted so they don't disrupt uptime or physical safety — the opposite priority ordering from most enterprise IT controls.

SP 800-53

The general-purpose federal security and privacy control catalog that SP 800-82's overlays are drawn from and tailor.

Architecture & Segmentation Guidance

SP 800-82's reference patterns for dividing an OT network into zones with controlled boundaries — the general-purpose counterpart to ship-specific zone requirements elsewhere in this course.

07

Application & Practical Use

SP 800-82 is most directly useful in this course wherever a system falls outside the scope of the ship-specific frameworks — chiefly shore-side port and terminal control systems.

Risk Register Structure

Provides the categorize–assess–monitor structure a port authority or terminal operator can use to build and maintain a defensible OT risk register.

Control Baseline Selection

Gives an OT owner a starting overlay to tailor, rather than requiring controls to be written from scratch or borrowed wholesale from an IT baseline.

Shore-Side Gap Filling

Applies to port cranes, terminal gate automation, and other shore-side OT that ship-specific frameworks like UR E26/E27 do not cover.

Common Reporting Language

The CSF alignment lets an OT program's status be reported using the same five-function language an organization's enterprise IT program already uses.

08

Classroom Uses

Overlay Tailoring Drill

Given a standard SP 800-53 control (for example, an automatic account-lockout policy), learners rewrite it as an OT overlay entry for a maritime constraint — such as a bridge system that cannot afford to lock out an operator mid-maneuver.

CSF Function Mapping

Given a fictional port terminal's existing security controls, learners map each control to one of the five CSF functions and identify which functions are under-represented.

Risk Management Process Walkthrough

Learners run a shore-side crane control system through the full categorize–select–implement–assess–authorize–monitor cycle, producing a one-page summary at each step.

Framework Boundary Exercise

Given a mixed list of shipboard and shore-side systems, learners sort each into "governed primarily by SP 800-82" versus "governed primarily by a ship-specific framework," and justify the split.
