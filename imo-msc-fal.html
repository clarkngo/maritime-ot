01

Foundations

MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3 provides high-level recommendations for maritime cyber risk management, jointly issued by IMO's Maritime Safety Committee and Facilitation Committee. Resolution MSC.428(98), adopted in 2017, is the binding instrument that does the real work: it affirms that an approved Safety Management System should already take cyber risk into account, per the existing risk-management requirements of the International Safety Management (ISM) Code. Together, the guideline explains what "addressing cyber risk" can look like, and the resolution makes doing so a compliance expectation.

Rising awareness of maritime cyber incidents through the mid-2010s — GPS and AIS spoofing reports, ransomware reaching shore-side systems, and growing dependence on networked navigation and cargo systems — pushed IMO to act. Rather than draft an entirely new maritime cybersecurity convention, IMO made a deliberate structural choice: fold cyber risk into the ISM Code's existing, already-mandatory Safety Management System framework. Every SOLAS vessel already had to have an approved SMS; MSC.428(98) simply clarified that "risk" in that existing framework includes cyber risk.

Three audiences, matching the ISM Code's own structure: shipowners and operators, who must update and maintain the SMS itself; Company Security Officers and Designated Persons Ashore (DPAs), who carry day-to-day responsibility for the SMS and, increasingly, for cyber risk visibility within it; and flag state administrations and Recognized Organizations, who audit the SMS during the Document of Compliance verification cycle and decide whether cyber risk has been adequately addressed.

It is the one framework in this set that applies to the entire existing SOLAS fleet immediately, regardless of build date — every vessel already carrying an approved SMS is already in scope. That is a sharp contrast with the IACS UR E26/E27 page's newbuild-only technical requirements. But the trade-off is depth: this is a governance-level obligation with no ship-design specifics of its own. It tells an operator that cyber risk must be visible in the SMS; it does not tell a shipyard how to segment a network or a system integrator how to harden a PLC.

03

Positioning: IMO MSC-FAL.1 / MSC.428(98) vs. the Other Core Frameworks

This is the only one of the four frameworks in this course that is immediately mandatory for the existing global fleet — enforced not through a design-time survey like a newbuild class notation, but through the routine flag-state ISM audit cycle every SOLAS vessel already goes through. Notably, it does not try to be self-sufficient: it explicitly expects organizations to draw on more technical frameworks — the IACS UR E26/E27 page for ship design, NIST SP 800-82 for general OT risk management, and MITRE ATT&CK for ICS for understanding real-world threat behavior — to actually satisfy the risk-management expectation it sets.

04

Core Structure

The instrument is really two documents working together: a non-binding guideline that explains the "what" and "how," and a short, binding resolution that makes addressing cyber risk part of an existing mandatory obligation. Neither document specifies ship-design controls of its own — both point outward, at the SMS process itself.

MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3Guidance
Guidelines on Maritime Cyber Risk Management
High-level, non-mandatory recommendations covering the elements of a cyber risk management approach: identify, protect, detect, respond, and recover — deliberately mirroring a general risk-management structure rather than prescribing specific technical controls.
Resolution MSC.428(98)Binding Requirement
Maritime Cyber Risk Management in Safety Management Systems
The short, binding text adopted in 2017 affirming that an approved Safety Management System should address cyber risk consistent with the ISM Code's existing objectives and functional requirements — with no cyber-specific technical annex of its own.

The SMS Integration Model

The conceptual core of this framework is that cyber risk is not meant to live in a standalone "cyber policy" document bolted onto the SMS — it should be visible across the SMS's existing functional requirements, the same structural elements every ISM-compliant company already maintains for physical and operational safety. The sub-cards below walk through how cyber risk conceptually maps onto that existing structure, followed by the enforcement mechanism that makes it a real compliance matter.

Governance

The company's safety and environmental protection policy, its statement of authority and responsibility, and its Designated Person Ashore (DPA) role should all conceptually extend to cyber risk — the same person or function that has visibility into a near-miss on deck should have visibility into a phishing incident or an unusual login on the cargo control network.

The master's overriding authority to act in the interest of safety, the resourcing and training of personnel, and the procedures governing key shipboard operations should each reflect cyber risk where relevant — a master empowered to isolate a compromised system and a crew trained to recognize a suspicious USB drive are cyber risk management operating through existing SMS channels, not a separate program.

Emergency preparedness procedures should account for a cyber-related casualty (loss of ECDIS, loss of propulsion control) alongside conventional emergencies, and the SMS's existing non-conformity and near-miss reporting channel should be able to capture and analyze a cyber event the same way it captures a safety near-miss — feeding lessons learned back into the SMS.

Maintenance of ship and equipment should extend to the software and configuration state of computer-based systems, not only their mechanical upkeep; SMS documentation should reflect how cyber risk is identified and managed; and the company's own internal verification, review, and evaluation of the SMS should periodically test whether that cyber risk visibility is real and current, not just written down once and forgotten.

The mechanism that turns this from aspiration into an audit matter is the annual verification of the company's Document of Compliance. IMO's guidance called for cyber risk to be addressed in the SMS by the first annual DOC verification after 1 January 2021 — meaning flag state administrations and Recognized Organizations conducting that routine audit are expected to check for cyber risk visibility across the SMS elements above, the same way they already check for safety and environmental compliance.

05

Definitions & Scope

These terms recur across both documents — learners who are shaky on the ISM Code's existing vocabulary will struggle to see how cyber risk actually attaches to it.

Safety Management System (SMS)

The documented system a shipping company must maintain under the ISM Code, covering policies, procedures, and responsibilities for safe ship operation and pollution prevention — the existing structure this framework asks cyber risk to be folded into.

ISM Code

The International Safety Management Code, the SOLAS-linked instrument that has required an approved SMS on covered vessels since long before cyber risk was explicitly named — MSC.428(98) affirms that its existing risk-management requirements already cover cyber risk.

Document of Compliance (DOC)

The certificate issued to a shipping company confirming its SMS meets ISM Code requirements, subject to annual verification — the audit event that operationalizes this framework's cyber risk expectation.

Designated Person Ashore (DPA)

The shore-based person named in the SMS with direct access to top management, responsible for monitoring the safety and pollution-prevention aspects of ship operation — increasingly expected to have cyber risk visibility as part of that role.

Company Security Officer (CSO)

The person designated under the ISPS Code responsible for developing and implementing a ship security assessment and plan — a role frequently paired with cyber risk oversight in practice, though the two frameworks originate separately.

Flag State Administration

The government under whose flag a vessel is registered, responsible (often via a Recognized Organization acting on its behalf) for verifying ISM Code compliance, including the DOC audit cycle this framework relies on for enforcement.

07

Application & Practical Use

Because this framework has already applied to the existing fleet since 2021, the practical work for a company is less about a one-time build project and more about ongoing SMS maintenance and audit readiness.

SMS Document Review & Update

Companies revisit existing SMS procedures — policy statements, emergency plans, maintenance procedures — to make cyber risk visible where it belongs, rather than adding an isolated cyber annex nobody else in the SMS ever reads.

DPA Monitoring Role

The Designated Person Ashore's existing monitoring function extends to tracking cyber risk indicators and incidents across the fleet, reporting them through the same channel used for safety non-conformities.

DOC Audit Preparation

Ahead of the annual Document of Compliance verification, companies prepare evidence that cyber risk is genuinely addressed across the SMS — not just a policy statement, but records showing the risk process is actually followed.

Shipboard Drills & Training

Existing emergency and safety drills are extended to include cyber scenarios — loss of ECDIS, a suspected malware infection — folding cyber preparedness into training crews already receive rather than creating a separate drill program.

08

Classroom Uses

SMS Gap Analysis

Given a fictional vessel's existing SMS documents (policy statement, emergency procedures, maintenance schedule, non-conformity log), learners identify where cyber risk is and isn't currently visible across the ISM Code's functional requirements, and draft the specific language needed to close each gap.

DOC Audit Prep Checklist

Learners build a practical checklist a company would use to prepare for its next annual Document of Compliance verification, covering each SMS element from Section 04 and the evidence a flag-state auditor would expect to see for each.

DPA Incident Report

Learners draft a short incident report a Designated Person Ashore would file after a fictional cyber event (a phishing email that led to a brief network isolation), modeled on how a physical safety near-miss would normally be reported through the same SMS channel.

Cross-Framework Bridge

Given the SMS gap analysis above, learners identify which gaps would be closed by adopting NIST SP 800-82's risk process, which would be closed by IACS UR E26/E27 design requirements (for a newbuild), and which are governance gaps that neither technical framework can fix on its own.
