Maritime OT Technologies & Field Devices

A device-by-device tour of maritime OT equipment — what each does, how it fails, and what it exposes on the network.

📊 Intermediate ⏱ 4 hours 🎯 5 objectives
Overview Navigation & Positioning Propulsion & Steering Cargo & Ballast Safety Instrumented Systems Network Exposure Activity Simulation Review

Lesson Overview

Lesson 2 gave you a vocabulary for reasoning about maritime OT — priorities, legal backbone, determinism, lifecycle, and a five-zone model. This lesson puts names and faces to that vocabulary. We walk the ship system by system: the devices that tell the bridge where the vessel is and what's around it, the devices that actually move the ship, the devices that manage cargo and ballast, and the devices whose entire job is to notice a hazard and act on it automatically. For each one, you will learn what it does, what happens when it fails, and — briefly, since Lesson 5 goes much deeper into the protocols themselves — what it typically exposes on the network.

Treat this lesson as building a mental inventory. By the time you reach Lesson 4's discussion of the IT protocols riding inside these networks, and Lesson 5's deep dive into maritime-specific protocols like NMEA and Modbus, you should already be able to picture, for any device named, roughly where it sits physically on a ship, how consequential its failure would be, and which of Lesson 2's five zones it belongs in.

Anchoring analogy

Think of this lesson as a hospital orientation tour rather than a lecture on hospital IT policy. You are walking the floors, learning what the imaging machine does, what the ventilator does, what the crash cart is for, and what happens to a patient if each one stops working — before anyone asks you to secure the hospital's network. The device tour has to come first, because you cannot reason about criticality, segmentation, or acceptable risk for a system you cannot picture.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Identify key navigation devices — ECDIS, radar/ARPA, GNSS, AIS, and the VDR — and describe what each does.
  • Identify key control devices — autopilot, ballast/cargo control, steering gear, and engine automation (AMS) — and describe what each does.
  • Identify key safety systems — fire/gas detection, emergency shutdown, and safety instrumented systems generally — and their role aboard ship.
  • Explain the failure modes of these devices and the operational consequences of each failure.
  • Explain, at a high level, what each device typically exposes on the network.

Navigation & positioning devices

≈ 35 min

The devices in this section exist to answer one question, continuously: where is the ship, and what else is out there?

ECDIS (Electronic Chart Display and Information System) is the mandated primary means of navigation on most SOLAS vessels — a networked electronic chart that plots the vessel's own position and overlays radar and AIS targets on top of it. When ECDIS fails, the crew falls back to backup arrangements: a secondary ECDIS unit, or on some vessels paper charts, with manual plotting slowing down fix-taking exactly when the crew can least afford the delay.

Radar/ARPA detects nearby vessels and obstacles by reflecting radio waves, and Automatic Radar Plotting Aid (ARPA) processing auto-tracks those targets to compute their course, speed, closest point of approach, and time to closest point of approach. Loss of ARPA tracking reverts the watch officer to manually plotting multiple targets by hand in restricted visibility — the exact workload radar and ARPA were introduced to remove.

A GNSS/GPS receiver provides a continuous position fix from a satellite constellation and feeds that position to ECDIS, AIS, the VDR, and other systems that assume it is correct. Its most dangerous failure mode is not an obvious outage but a silent one: a jammed or spoofed signal can feed a wrong position to every downstream system that trusts it, with no automatic alarm unless someone cross-checks against an independent source.

The gyrocompass provides true heading independent of magnetic variation, feeding heading data to the autopilot, ARPA, and AIS. A drifting or failed gyrocompass introduces a systematic heading error that silently propagates into every system depending on it — including the autopilot's course-keeping.

The AIS transponder (Automatic Identification System) broadcasts the vessel's identity, position, course, and speed to nearby ships and shore stations over VHF, and receives the same broadcasts from others. An undetected malfunction, deliberate "AIS dark" behavior, or spoofing of AIS data removes the vessel from other ships' — and shore authorities' — situational picture, or worse, can be exploited to impersonate a different vessel entirely.

The VDR (Voyage Data Recorder) is the maritime industry's "black box" — it continuously records bridge audio, radar imagery, ECDIS data, and machinery and helm parameters, purpose-built for post-incident investigation rather than day-to-day operation. A VDR outage or a tampered recording does not affect the ship in the moment, but it removes the evidentiary record an investigation would need after a casualty.

Worked example

During an approach to a busy strait, a container ship's GNSS receiver began reporting a position roughly 300 meters off the vessel's actual location — a plausible signature of GPS spoofing rather than a hard failure, since the receiver kept producing what looked like a normal fix. The error propagated silently into ECDIS and AIS until the second officer noticed the displayed position didn't match a visual bearing on a charted landmark. The incident underscored why cross-checking GNSS output against an independent source — radar, visual bearings, or a second receiver — remains a required watchkeeping practice rather than a redundant one.

Propulsion & steering control

≈ 35 min

Where Section 1's devices tell the ship where it is, this section's devices actually move it.

The autopilot / course computer automatically holds a commanded heading or track using gyrocompass and GNSS input, issuing continuous correction commands to the steering gear. An undetected autopilot fault can produce a slow, gradual drift off track long before anyone notices — particularly on a quiet watch with few other cues to prompt a manual check.

Steering gear control translates helm or autopilot commands into hydraulic ram movement of the rudder itself. Loss of steering gear control is one of the most serious failures a ship can have underway, which is exactly why SOLAS requires an independent, physically separate backup steering control arrangement and posted emergency steering procedures — this is not a system the industry is willing to leave single-string.

The engine automation system (AMS) monitors and controls the main engine and auxiliary machinery, consolidating alarms for engine-room parameters — temperatures, pressures, RPM — onto a single console. An AMS outage removes centralized alarm visibility, forcing engineers back to local gauges and manual rounds exactly when something in the machinery space may be going wrong.

A controllable-pitch propeller (CPP) control system adjusts propeller blade pitch to vary thrust independent of shaft RPM, letting the engine run at a constant, efficient speed while thrust changes. A CPP control fault can leave the propeller pitched for continued forward thrust with no ability to reduce speed quickly — a serious hazard during close-quarters maneuvering or an emergency stop.

Bow and stern thrusters provide lateral thrust for docking and low-speed maneuvering, when the rudder alone can't generate enough sideways force. Thruster failure during a berthing maneuver removes a maneuvering option the pilot may already be actively relying on, particularly in confined waters or a stiff crosswind.

Anchoring analogy

If navigation devices are the ship's eyes, propulsion and steering devices are its muscles and tendons — and just like a human body, a fault doesn't have to be total to be dangerous. A knee that still bends but can no longer bear full weight is a real hazard even though it "still works"; a CPP control that still changes pitch but with a growing lag is the same kind of degraded-but-not-obviously-broken failure.

Worked example

A ferry's primary steering gear control lost hydraulic pressure during a routine crossing. The bridge team detected the loss within seconds via a steering gear alarm, declared an emergency, and switched command to the independent secondary steering system per the posted emergency steering procedure — a drill the crew had practiced the previous month. The vessel completed the crossing on backup steering without incident, which is precisely the outcome the SOLAS-mandated redundancy requirement exists to guarantee.

Cargo & ballast systems

≈ 35 min

These systems manage what's inside the ship — cargo and ballast water — rather than how the ship moves through the water.

The ballast control system manages the pumping and valving of seawater ballast between tanks to control the vessel's trim, list, and stability, particularly during loading and discharge when the vessel's weight distribution is changing. An uncommanded or stuck ballast valve can shift trim or stability beyond safe limits, especially if it happens during active cargo operations when several tanks may already be mid-transfer.

On tankers and bulkers, the cargo control system centralizes monitoring and control of cargo tank valves, pumps, and — on tankers — the inert gas system, for loading and discharge operations. Loss of cargo control visibility during a loading operation can allow a tank overfill to develop before a manual check catches it.

Cargo pump control governs the speed and sequencing of cargo pumps during loading and discharge. An uncontrolled or unexpected pump speed change can create pressure surges in cargo piping, risking a spill or structural stress on the pipework.

Tank level and pressure sensors instrument individual ballast or cargo tanks and feed their readings to the ballast and cargo control systems and the high-level alarms those systems drive. A failed or slowly drifting level sensor can silently defeat the very high-level alarm it feeds — the same failure chain behind the ballast-pump alarm scenario worked through in Lesson 2.

Worked example

A bulk carrier's ballast control system received a valve-open command during deballasting that never fully executed due to a mechanical actuator fault — the control panel showed the valve as open, but it had only partially opened. The mismatch between commanded and actual tank levels wasn't caught until the chief officer's routine sounding round found the discrepancy, roughly forty minutes after the command was issued. The vessel's stability was never in serious jeopardy, but the incident prompted a fleet-wide review of whether ballast control feedback (valve position, not just valve command) should be displayed more prominently on the console.

Safety instrumented systems

≈ 30 min

Every device covered so far exists to help the crew do something — navigate, propel, manage cargo. The devices in this section exist for a narrower and more absolute purpose: detect a hazardous condition and act, automatically, independent of whatever else is happening on the bridge or in the engine room. A Safety Instrumented System (SIS) is the general name for that category — fire and gas detection, emergency shutdown systems, and, in a broader sense, the general alarm and watertight door systems all function as an SIS. These are the highest-criticality, most deliberately fail-safe-oriented systems on the ship.

Fire and gas detection continuously monitors spaces for smoke, heat, flame, or combustible and toxic gas concentration, triggering alarms and, where configured, automatic suppression. An undetected sensor fault can leave a compartment effectively unmonitored while the panel continues to display normal status — a failure that is dangerous precisely because it is invisible until tested.

Emergency shutdown (ESD) systems automatically isolate fuel, cargo, or ventilation systems the instant a hazardous condition is detected, independent of normal operator control. A false trip halts cargo or propulsion operations unnecessarily and has a real operational cost; a failed-to-trip ESD leaves a genuine hazard unmitigated and has a far worse one. Both directions carry consequences, which is exactly why ESD logic is deliberately conservative and engineered to be fail-safe.

The general alarm system is the ship-wide audible and visual alarm used to summon the crew to emergency stations. A general alarm failure during a genuine emergency directly delays crew muster, with immediate consequences for survivability.

Watertight door control remotely operates and monitors the watertight doors that preserve a vessel's subdivision and buoyancy after hull damage. A door that fails to close — or worse, shows "closed" on the indicator panel while actually standing open — defeats the exact damage-control function the door exists to provide.

Safety instrumented systems are engineered around fail-safe defaults: when a component fails, the system is designed to fail toward the safer state — an ESD valve that fails closed, not open. Any maritime OT security control that touches these systems has to preserve that fail-safe property above every other consideration, which ties directly back to Lesson 2's priority stack: these are the systems with the least tolerance for an availability compromise, but also the least tolerance for a security control that could suppress or delay their own alarm output.

Teaching note

Learners sometimes assume "highest criticality" means "most heavily secured" in the conventional IT sense — lots of authentication, lots of logging, lots of restriction. Push back on that instinct here: an SIS's defining security property is that it keeps functioning and keeps failing safely, which sometimes argues for less friction on its control path, not more. Revisit this tension when Lesson 8 covers how security frameworks get adapted for OT rather than applied unmodified.

What each device exposes on the network

≈ 25 min

Every device covered in this lesson eventually talks to something else — a display, a logger, a controller, or another sensor — and that conversation happens over some physical interface, using some protocol, at some point on the vessel's network. The table below is a summary reference, not a deep technical treatment; Lesson 4 covers the IT protocols riding inside shipboard and port networks, and Lesson 5 goes much deeper into maritime-specific protocols like NMEA 0183/2000 and Modbus. What matters here is the pattern: the more safety-critical a device is, the more likely it sits deep in Lesson 2's zone model (Zone 1) on a protocol that was never designed with authentication in mind — because it was designed for a wire nobody else was ever supposed to be on.

Teaching note

Point learners back to Lesson 1's discussion of serial versus Ethernet at this table: notice how many of the highest-criticality rows still rely on a serial or hardwired fieldbus connection rather than Ethernet. That is not an oversight — it is often a deliberate choice, since a dedicated point-to-point link is harder for an unrelated device to reach than a shared switched network, even without any authentication at all.

Key Terms
  • ECDISElectronic Chart Display and Information System — the mandated electronic replacement for paper charts as the primary means of navigation.
  • AISAutomatic Identification System — a VHF-based transponder broadcasting vessel identity, position, course, and speed to nearby ships and shore stations.
  • VDRVoyage Data Recorder — the maritime "black box," continuously recording bridge audio, radar, ECDIS, and machinery data for post-incident investigation.
  • Ballast ControlThe system of pumps, valves, and sensors that manages ballast water distribution to maintain a vessel's trim, list, and stability.
  • SISSafety Instrumented System — a device such as fire/gas detection or an ESD unit, engineered to detect a hazard and drive the process to a fail-safe state independent of normal operator control.
