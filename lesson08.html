Frameworks for Maritime OT Security

IACS UR E26/E27 cyber resilience requirements, IMO's maritime cyber risk guidelines, NIST SP 800-82, and IEC 62443 — how they overlap, where they differ, and how to pick one spine for a course instead of teaching four at once.

📊 Intermediate ⏱ 4 hours 🎯 5 objectives

Lesson Overview

Ask four different people in maritime cybersecurity which framework matters most and you will likely get four different, confidently held answers — and all four could be defensible, because they are usually answering slightly different questions. A shipyard project manager quoting a newbuild contract needs a design requirement with teeth. A superintendent preparing for a flag-state audit needs a document that maps to the vessel's Safety Management System. A port authority building a shore-side OT security program needs a general risk-management vocabulary that has nothing to do with vessels at all. A threat analyst trying to understand how a ransomware crew actually breaches an engine control network needs none of the above — they need a catalog of real adversary behavior.

This lesson introduces the four frameworks a maritime OT security program most commonly encounters — IACS UR E26 & E27, IMO MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3 and Resolution MSC.428(98), NIST SP 800-82, and MITRE ATT&CK for ICS — not to teach any one of them to reference depth (each has its own dedicated page on this site for that), but to teach the map: what kind of instrument each one is, what problem it solves, where they reinforce each other, and — critically — how to choose one as your primary spine instead of trying to comply with, or teach, all four with equal weight in a single sitting.

Anchoring analogy

Think of these four frameworks the way you would think of a building's fire code, its insurance policy, a national fire-safety handbook, and a database of how fires have actually started in similar buildings. The fire code (IACS UR E26/E27) is a mandatory design requirement enforced at construction. The insurance policy's safety clauses (IMO MSC-FAL.1/MSC.428(98)) get audited against your existing operating procedures, not the building's architecture. The national handbook (NIST SP 800-82, alongside IEC 62443's zone concepts) gives you shared vocabulary and general good practice that applies whether or not this specific building was ever inspected under the fire code. And the incident database (MITRE ATT&CK for ICS) tells you how fires like this one actually start, so you know what to check for. You would not read all four documents with equal urgency on day one of owning the building — you would pick the one that answers the question in front of you.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Identify the four core frameworks covered in this lesson and classify each by type — mandatory class requirement, regulatory guideline, federal guidance, or general design standard.
  • Explain what specific problem each framework was built to solve.
  • Explain how the frameworks overlap and reinforce each other, including the shared zones-and-conduits concept that appears in both IEC 62443 and IACS UR E26.
  • Evaluate which framework is the right primary reference for a given organizational scenario.
  • Explain why a course or organization should pick one framework as its primary spine rather than attempting to teach or comply with all four with equal depth.
Part A · Foundations Lecture
🗺️

Four frameworks, four different jobs

≈ 30 min

The four frameworks this lesson covers did not emerge from a single coordinated effort. Each was written by a different body, for a different audience, to solve a different problem, and each has its own dedicated reference page on this site — IACS UR E26 & E27, IMO MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3 & Resolution MSC.428(98), NIST SP 800-82 Rev. 3, and MITRE ATT&CK for ICS — that you should treat as the deep-dive companion to the brief orientation given here.

IACS UR E26/E27 is a mandatory unified requirement issued by the International Association of Classification Societies. It is a design instrument: it tells a shipyard and its system integrators what cyber resilience properties a new vessel's networks and onboard computer-based systems must have, and it is enforced the same way structural and stability requirements are — through classification society plan approval, survey, and certification during newbuild.

IMO MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3 and Resolution MSC.428(98) is a regulatory guideline from the International Maritime Organization. Rather than specifying network architecture, it requires that cyber risk be addressed within a vessel's existing Safety Management System (SMS) under the ISM Code — making it an operational and audit instrument, not a design one, and one that applies to essentially the entire SOLAS-regulated fleet, new and existing alike.

NIST SP 800-82 is general U.S. federal guidance on operational technology security. It was never written with ships in mind — its examples run toward power grids, water treatment, and manufacturing — but its risk-management structure and control vocabulary are broadly portable, and it is widely adopted by port authorities and terminal operators who have no maritime-specific OT standard of their own to reach for.

MITRE ATT&CK for ICS is different in kind from the other three: it is not a compliance framework at all, but a descriptive, continuously updated knowledge base of real-world adversary tactics and techniques against industrial control systems. It answers "how do attacks like this actually happen," which none of the other three frameworks are designed to do.

IACS UR E26/E27 in brief

≈ 30 min

IACS Unified Requirements E26 and E27 split cyber resilience into two complementary levels. UR E26 addresses the ship as a whole — network segmentation into security zones, boundary protection between zones, remote access control, and resilience of the vessel's overall computer-based system architecture. UR E27 addresses individual onboard computer-based systems — the design, development, and testing requirements each system and its supplier must meet, including secure configuration, malware defense, and the ability to recover from a cyber incident.

Both requirements apply to new ships contracted for construction from 1 July 2024 onward, and both work in security zones — logically and physically grouped systems that share a boundary and a consistent set of protections — organized around a set of capability categories (identify, protect, detect, respond, recover — deliberately mirroring the well-known NIST Cybersecurity Framework functions) that each zone and system must demonstrate.

Worked example

A shipyard building a container vessel under a contract signed in September 2024 must show its classification society plan approval that the propulsion control zone, the navigation zone, and the crew Wi-Fi zone are each separately defined, that the boundaries between them are protected and monitored, and that individual systems within each zone — the ECDIS, the engine control system — meet UR E27's system-level design and testing requirements. This is enforced the same way hull strength calculations are: no approved plan, no class certificate, no delivery.

This lesson deliberately stays at the orientation level. For zone definitions, the full capability-category breakdown, and applicability edge cases, see the dedicated IACS UR E26 & E27 reference page.

IMO MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3 and Resolution MSC.428(98) in brief

≈ 30 min

Where IACS UR E26/E27 is a design requirement enforced at newbuild, the IMO's approach took a different, older instrument and pointed it at a new problem. Resolution MSC.428(98), adopted in 2017, affirmed that existing risk management requirements already in the ISM Code apply to cyber risk — it did not create a new, separate compliance regime. MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3 is the accompanying guidance that spells out what that means in practice: cyber risk should be identified, assessed, and managed within the vessel's Safety Management System (SMS), the same document set already used to manage every other operational risk aboard.

This is precisely why MSC-FAL.1/MSC.428(98) matters for the whole SOLAS-regulated fleet, not just new construction: an SMS audit happens every year, on every certified vessel, whether it was built in 1998 or 2025, and whether or not it falls under the newer IACS UR E26/E27 newbuild requirements. Folding cyber risk into the SMS made it a routine, recurring flag-state audit matter rather than a one-time design checkbox — which is also why it took effect at the first annual SMS verification after 1 January 2021, giving operators a lead-in period to update their SMS documentation rather than facing an audit finding on day one.

Anchoring analogy

If UR E26/E27 is a building code enforced once, at construction, MSC.428(98) is closer to a fire marshal who now checks your existing fire-safety plan every year — not because the building changed, but because the definition of what a responsible fire-safety plan has to cover changed.

Full audit-cycle mechanics and the relationship to the ISPS Code are covered in Lesson 9; for the guidance text itself, see the dedicated IMO Maritime Cyber Risk Management reference page.

NIST SP 800-82 and IEC 62443 as general-purpose reinforcement

≈ 35 min

Neither IACS UR E26/E27 nor IMO MSC-FAL.1 was written with a container terminal's crane control network or a port authority's shore-side SCADA system in mind — both are fundamentally vessel-centric instruments. That gap is exactly where NIST SP 800-82 and IEC 62443 earn their keep. Neither is maritime-specific, and neither is mandatory in most jurisdictions, but both supply something the maritime-specific frameworks don't: a general-purpose vocabulary and structure for OT risk management and secure network design that applies just as well to a shore-side terminal as it does to a chemical plant.

NIST SP 800-82 gives a maritime OT program a shared risk-management structure — asset identification, threat and vulnerability characterization, risk assessment, and a control catalog — that a port or terminal can adopt wholesale even though it will never see a class surveyor. IEC 62443 contributes something more specific: the zones-and-conduits design vocabulary, which should sound familiar — it's the same conceptual pattern IACS UR E26 uses for shipboard security zones, just generalized to any industrial network. A security architect who learns zones-and-conduits from IEC 62443 while working on a shore terminal can carry that exact mental model onto a ship's UR E26 zone diagram without relearning the concept from scratch, and vice versa.

Worked example

A port authority operating container-handling cranes, gate systems, and terminal operating system servers has no IACS UR E26/E27 obligation — none of that equipment is a vessel — and no ISM Code SMS to fold cyber risk into. Its security team instead builds its OT risk register directly on NIST SP 800-82's structure, and designs its network segmentation using IEC 62443 zones and conduits: a crane-control zone, a gate-automation zone, and a business-IT zone, each separated by a documented, monitored conduit. When that same team later works with a shipping line on ship-to-shore data exchange, the zones-and-conduits vocabulary they already use maps directly onto the vessel side's UR E26 zones.

Teaching note

This is a good moment to name the overlap explicitly rather than let learners discover it on their own: zones-and-conduits is not two competing ideas, it's one idea that shows up in both a mandatory ship-design requirement (UR E26) and a voluntary general-purpose standard (IEC 62443). Frameworks reinforce each other more often than they compete — this module is the clearest example of it in the lesson.

Part B · Applied Practice
🧭

Choosing a spine: how to pick one framework as primary

≈ 30 min

An instructor designing a course, or a security manager standing up a real program, faces the same practical problem: there is not enough time or budget to teach or comply with all four frameworks with equal depth, and trying to do so tends to produce shallow familiarity with everything and working competence with nothing. The fix is to pick a primary spine — the one framework that structures your program's core documentation and decisions — and treat the other three as reference material you pull in when the spine framework's scope runs out.

A simple decision rule covers most real situations:

  • Securing a newbuild vessel? Start with IACS UR E26/E27 — it's the mandatory design requirement your shipyard and class society will already be enforcing.
  • Auditing an existing fleet's Safety Management System? Start with IMO MSC-FAL.1/MSC.428(98) — it's the instrument your flag-state auditor is actually checking against.
  • Building a general OT security program spanning ships and shore-side ports? Start with NIST SP 800-82 for overall structure, and pull in IEC 62443's zones-and-conduits vocabulary for network design.
  • Threat-modeling a specific incident or system? Pull in MITRE ATT&CK for ICS regardless of which spine you chose — it's a reference tool for that one job, not a competing spine.

None of this means the other three frameworks disappear from view — a newbuild program built on UR E26/E27 still benefits from ATT&CK-informed threat modeling, and a port program built on NIST SP 800-82 still needs to understand MSC.428(98) if it ever handles vessel-facing systems. Choosing a spine is about where you start and what structures your primary documentation, not about ignoring the rest.

Teaching note

If you only have time to leave learners with one takeaway from this lesson, make it this: the question is never "which framework is correct," it's "which framework answers the question I actually have right now." A learner who leaves able to route a scenario to the right framework has learned more than one who has memorized all four in equal, shallow depth.
