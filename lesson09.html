Governance, Risk & Compliance for Maritime IT/OT

Ship Security Plans, the ISM Code's cyber risk requirements, flag state and port state control expectations, classification society audits, and the asset inventory and risk register that make an audit survivable.

📊 Intermediate ⏱ 4 hours 🎯 5 objectives

Lesson Overview

A framework only becomes real for a ship's crew and shore staff once it shows up as a document someone has to produce, a plan someone has to follow, and an inspector someone has to satisfy. Lesson 8 introduced the frameworks; this lesson walks through the paper trail and the people who check it — the Ship Security Plan required under the ISPS Code, the way cyber risk was folded into the ISM Code's Safety Management System, the roles of flag state and port state control, the classification society's dual audit and Recognized Organization role, and — because none of this works without one — the basic asset inventory and risk register that give an operator something concrete to hand an auditor.

None of this is abstract compliance theater. A vessel that cannot produce a current network diagram, a defensible asset inventory, or evidence that cyber risk was actually discussed at its last SMS review is a vessel that is going to generate audit findings — and, more importantly, is a vessel whose security team does not actually know what it is defending. The paperwork and the security posture are not separate concerns; a good risk register is a security tool that happens to also satisfy an auditor.

Anchoring analogy

Think of a vessel's compliance documentation the way you would think of a patient's medical chart. The chart doesn't cure anything by itself — but a hospital that can't produce an accurate, current chart during an inspection has a real problem, and so does a hospital where the chart says one thing and the patient's actual condition says another. A Ship Security Plan, an SMS cyber risk entry, and an asset inventory are that ship's chart: they need to be current, accurate, and — the part most programs get wrong — they need to actually match what a technician would find if they walked the network today.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain the Ship Security Plan (SSP) and its relationship to the ISPS Code.
  • Explain how cyber risk is folded into the ISM Code's Safety Management System.
  • Describe flag state and port state control's role in maritime cyber compliance.
  • Describe the classification society's audit and survey role.
  • Build a basic asset inventory and risk register appropriate for a vessel or fleet.
Part A · Foundations Lecture

The ISPS Code and the Ship Security Plan

≈ 30 min

The International Ship and Port Facility Security (ISPS) Code entered into force in 2004, adopted as a direct response to the post-9/11 recognition that ships and port facilities were vulnerable to deliberate, unlawful acts — originally understood almost entirely in physical terms: unauthorized boarding, smuggling, sabotage, piracy, and terrorism. Every SOLAS-regulated vessel above the applicable size threshold must carry an approved Ship Security Plan (SSP), developed under the direction of a designated Ship Security Officer (SSO) aboard and a Company Security Officer (CSO) ashore, describing the security measures, procedures, and equipment in place to prevent those unlawful acts.

For its first decade and more, the SSP's contents stayed almost entirely physical: restricted-area access control, gangway watch procedures, security drills, and coordination with port facility security officers. That has changed. As vessel systems converged with shore networks (Lesson 1) and cyber risk became recognized as a route to the same kinds of consequences the ISPS Code was written to prevent — a manipulated cargo system, a disabled propulsion control, an falsified AIS track — SSPs increasingly need to address cyber risk explicitly, alongside physical security, rather than leaving it entirely to the separate SMS cyber risk management process covered in Module 2.

Worked example

A container line's Company Security Officer updates the fleet's standard SSP template to add a short cyber-security annex: who to notify if the cargo management system behaves unexpectedly, what to do if a USB device of unknown origin is found near the bridge, and how a suspected cyber incident should be logged alongside the physical security incidents the SSP already tracks. This doesn't replace the SMS-based cyber risk management process in Module 2 — it makes sure the ship's frontline security officer, who may not be the same person managing the SMS, has a documented, drillable procedure for the cyber scenarios most likely to look like a security incident in the moment.

Teaching note

Learners sometimes assume the SSP and the SMS cyber risk requirements (Module 2) are the same document, or that one supersedes the other. They are two separate instruments under two separate IMO codes (ISPS and ISM respectively), maintained by roles that may or may not be the same person aboard a given vessel — worth stating plainly before moving on.

📘

The ISM Code, the SMS, and MSC.428(98)

≈ 30 min

The International Safety Management (ISM) Code requires every certified vessel and its managing company to maintain a Safety Management System (SMS) — the documented set of policies, procedures, and risk-assessment processes covering everything from maintenance to emergency response. When the IMO's Maritime Safety Committee turned to cyber risk, it made a deliberate, consequential choice: rather than writing an entirely new cyber-specific code and certification scheme, Resolution MSC.428(98) affirmed that cyber risk already falls within the risk management the ISM Code requires, and directed that it be addressed within each company's existing SMS.

That choice matters practically. It means a maritime cyber risk program does not get a separate audit calendar, a separate certificate, or a separate officer role by default — it gets folded into a process every operator was already required to run. The accompanying guidance, MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3, spells out what "addressed within the SMS" should actually look like: risk identification covering both IT and OT systems, defined roles and responsibilities, contingency planning, and response and recovery procedures, documented the same way any other operational risk in the SMS is documented.

Anchoring analogy

Rather than issuing every vessel a brand-new "cyber license," the IMO effectively said: your existing safety inspector's checklist now has a cyber section on it. The inspector doesn't change, the certificate doesn't change — but what counts as a complete, well-run safety management system does.

For the full text of MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3 and Resolution MSC.428(98), and a deeper walkthrough of how they were adopted, see the dedicated IMO Maritime Cyber Risk Management reference page.

Flag state and port state control

≈ 30 min

A vessel's flag state — the country whose flag it flies and under whose law it is registered — bears the primary responsibility for ensuring the vessel meets its international obligations, including ISM Code compliance. In practice, flag states routinely delegate SMS audits to a classification society acting as a Recognized Organization (RO) (covered in Module 4), but accountability for the outcome remains with the flag state. Since cyber risk was folded into the SMS by MSC.428(98), it has been checked as part of that same audit cycle — specifically from the first annual SMS verification carried out after 1 January 2021, the date the IMO set for cyber risk management to be incorporated into SMS audits fleet-wide.

Port state control (PSC) works differently. Where flag state oversight follows the vessel across its whole operating life, PSC is an inspection regime carried out by the authorities of a port a foreign-flagged vessel calls at, checking that the ship meets applicable international conventions regardless of which flag it flies. A PSC officer is not typically conducting a full cyber audit at the gangway — but they can, and increasingly do, ask to see documentary evidence that cyber risk is being managed: a current SMS entry addressing cyber risk, crew familiarity with basic incident reporting procedures, and evidence that the ship's documented cyber risk management reflects reality rather than a template nobody has updated since it was issued.

CharacteristicFlag StatePort State Control
Authority basisThe vessel's own registry and national lawThe port country's authority over foreign vessels calling there

Worked example

A bulk carrier flagged in one country calls at a foreign port. The port state control officer, reviewing the vessel's SMS documentation, asks the master to show where cyber risk is addressed and whether the crew can describe the procedure for reporting a suspected cyber incident. The master produces the SMS cyber risk section, but it references a network diagram that is visibly out of date — a satellite communications upgrade installed eighteen months earlier isn't reflected anywhere in the documentation. The officer notes it as a deficiency requiring correction, not a detainable offense on its own, but a clear signal that the SMS entry was written once and never revisited.

Classification society audits

≈ 35 min

A classification society plays two related but distinct roles that are easy to conflate. First, it verifies compliance with its own class rules — the technical requirements a vessel must meet to earn and keep its class certificate, which for covered newbuilds now include IACS UR E26 and E27's cyber resilience design requirements, checked through plan approval at design stage and survey during and after construction. Second, in many jurisdictions the same society acts as a Recognized Organization (RO), performing statutory audits — including ISM Code and SMS audits — on the flag state's behalf, under authority the flag state has delegated to it.

These two roles use different rulebooks and different triggers, even when the same surveyor is doing both jobs on the same visit: class survey asks "does this system meet the class society's technical rules," while a statutory RO audit asks "does this operator meet the flag state's regulatory requirements." A gap found in one doesn't automatically become a finding in the other, though in practice a well-run vessel keeps both aligned.

Worked example — a class survey finding

During a periodic class survey, a surveyor reviewing a vessel's network architecture documentation against its UR E26 zone diagram finds an undocumented network change: a new remote-diagnostics connection installed by an engine vendor's technician during a prior port call, wired directly into the propulsion control zone without going through the documented boundary protection or appearing anywhere in the as-built diagram. The surveyor raises a finding requiring the operator to either remove the connection, document and properly secure it within the approved zone architecture, or provide a justified variance — because an undocumented path into a zone that UR E26 explicitly required to be protected is exactly the kind of drift the requirement exists to catch. The finding closes only once the ship's actual network matches its documentation again, not before.

Teaching note

The worked example above is a good bridge to Module 5: a class surveyor could not have caught that undocumented connection without an accurate baseline to compare against. Every governance and audit mechanism covered in this lesson — SSP, SMS, flag state, PSC, class — ultimately depends on the same underlying artifact: a current, accurate record of what's actually on the network. That's exactly what Module 5 builds.

Building an asset inventory and risk register

≈ 35 min

Every compliance mechanism covered so far — the SSP, the SMS cyber risk entry, the flag state audit, the PSC inspection, the class survey — is only as good as the underlying record of what equipment actually exists on the vessel and what could go wrong with it. Two artifacts do that work: the asset inventory and the risk register.

A maritime OT asset inventory should capture, at minimum: the device itself (make, model, function), the zone it sits in (matching the vessel's UR E26 or equivalent zone architecture), its criticality (what happens if it fails or is compromised), the vendor and support contact, the firmware or software version currently installed, and the last patch or dry-dock service date — because OT equipment is frequently only updated during scheduled yard periods, unlike shore IT which can patch on a rolling basis.

A risk register entry, in turn, should be structured around: a clear risk description, an estimated likelihood, an estimated consequence, the existing controls already in place, a treatment plan for anything not yet adequately controlled, an accountable owner, and a review date — ideally tied into a POA&M-style (Plan of Action and Milestones) tracking process so that treatment plans have real deadlines and someone checks whether they were actually completed, rather than a register that gets written once and never revisited, exactly like the SMS cyber risk entry in the port state control worked example above.

Anchoring analogy

An asset inventory without a risk register is like a hospital's equipment log with no chart of which patients are in critical condition — you know what you have, but not what to worry about first. A risk register without an asset inventory is worse: you're prioritizing risks against equipment you can't actually verify still exists, still has that configuration, or is even still connected the way the register assumes. They only work as a pair.
