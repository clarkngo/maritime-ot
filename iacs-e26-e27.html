01

Foundations

UR E26 and UR E27 are Unified Requirements issued by IACS, the umbrella body whose member classification societies (DNV, ABS, Lloyd's Register, ClassNK, and others) jointly agree on baseline technical rules. Unlike most of the frameworks in this course, an IACS UR is not voluntary guidance — once adopted, each member society writes it into its own class rules, and a ship cannot obtain or keep class without meeting it.

Through the 2010s, IMO's Resolution MSC.428(98) put maritime cyber risk inside the ISM Code's Safety Management System (see Lesson 9 and the IMO framework page), but that resolution set an expectation without specifying technical design requirements. Rising ransomware incidents against ports and carriers, growing reliance on integrated bridge and engine-control systems, and pressure from flag states and insurers pushed IACS to develop ship- and system-level technical requirements that shipyards and equipment makers could actually design and build to — resulting in UR E26 and UR E27.

The two requirements split responsibility much the way IEC 62443 splits its series: shipyards and system integrators who design and build the vessel's overall network architecture work primarily to E26; equipment manufacturers (ECDIS, engine control, ballast control, and similar computer-based systems) work primarily to E27; and shipowners and operators inherit both once the vessel is delivered and must maintain the cyber resilience the ship was built with.

It's the only framework in this set that is both written specifically for ships and mandatory by default for the ships it covers — a newbuild simply cannot be classed without meeting it. NIST SP 800-82 is voluntary guidance; ATT&CK for ICS is descriptive; the IMO instrument sets a management-system expectation without ship-design specifics. UR E26/E27 is the technical design requirement that gives a shipyard something concrete to build to and a surveyor something concrete to verify.

03

Positioning: UR E26/E27 vs. the Other Core Frameworks

The IMO instrument tells an operator that cyber risk must be managed inside the ship's Safety Management System — but it doesn't specify how the ship's network should be built. NIST SP 800-82 offers general OT risk guidance not written for ships specifically. ATT&CK for ICS documents what attackers actually do to systems like these once built. UR E26/E27 is the one framework in the set that answers "how does a shipyard design and build a cyber-resilient vessel, and how does a surveyor verify it?"

04

Ship-Level & System-Level Structure

Two pieces of vocabulary — Security Zones and Computer-Based Systems — cut across both requirements and get defined before anything else. Everything after that lives in one of two linked documents, organized by the level at which the requirement applies. The stage colors below reuse this site's existing strand colors: Ship-Level borrows the amber used for "Foundations," and System-Level borrows the blue used for "Protocols."

The Core Architectural Idea: Security Zones

UR E26 requires the vessel's onboard network to be divided into documented security zones, each grouped by function and criticality — navigation, propulsion and machinery control, cargo and ballast control, and general/administrative (including crew Wi-Fi) are typical groupings on a real vessel. Each zone boundary must have defined, verifiable protection, and traffic between zones must be controlled and, where warranted, monitored — the same zones-and-conduits logic Lesson 8 introduces through IEC 62443, applied specifically to a ship's own systems.

Capability Categories (System Level)

ConceptWhat It Captures
CBSComputer-Based System — any programmable system with digital interfaces onboard, from ECDIS to a ballast control PLC, in scope for E27 requirements
Cat.Each in-scope system is assessed for the severity of consequence if compromised, and cyber resilience capabilities are scaled to that severity — a navigation or propulsion-critical system is held to a materially higher bar than an administrative workstation

Verifying a system's actual capability against its assigned severity is analogous to the SL-T/SL-A gap check in IEC 62443 — the most common finding in a newbuild survey is a system whose delivered capability doesn't yet match what its criticality demands.

Whole-vessel requirements: security zone architecture, network segmentation, remote access control, backup and recovery capability, and a cyber resilience test program verified before delivery.

Cyber Resilience of Onboard Systems & Equipment
Requirements for the individual computer-based systems that sit inside those zones: hardening, malware protection, secure configuration, and capability scaled to the severity category assigned to that system.

Ship-Level Requirement Areas (UR E26)

Foundational

Requires a documented network diagram showing every security zone, every boundary device, and every conduit between zones — the artifact a surveyor reviews first. This is the maritime equivalent of the zone diagram Lesson 8's IEC 62443 exercise has learners redraw.

Any remote connection from shore — an OEM diagnosing a bridge system, a shore office pulling engine data — must be controlled, logged, and disable-able from onboard. This directly anticipates the remote-vendor-access scenarios covered in Lesson 10.

Requires a documented, tested means of recovering critical systems after a cyber incident — reflecting the reality Lesson 11 covers: a ship mid-voyage cannot simply wait for a shore IT team to arrive.

Requires testing of the integrated ship system before delivery — verification that zone boundaries, remote access controls, and recovery procedures actually work as designed, not just as documented.

System-Level Requirement Areas (UR E27)

Removing unused services, closing unnecessary ports, and eliminating default credentials on the individual computer-based system — the equipment-maker-facing counterpart to the segmentation work E26 does at the network level.

Capability scaled to the system's severity category — a navigation-critical CBS is held to materially stronger malware-protection requirements than a lower-criticality administrative system.

Requires the manufacturer to define a supportable patching process for the life of the system — directly addressing the dry-dock-length patch window problem Lesson 7 covers.

05

Definitions & Scope

These terms recur across both requirements — learners who are shaky on them will misapply the zone and category concepts above almost immediately.

Computer-Based System (CBS)

Any programmable, digitally interfaced system onboard — ECDIS, autopilot, ballast or cargo control, engine automation — that falls in scope of E27's system-level requirements.

Security Zone

A grouping of onboard systems that share a common function and criticality, separated from other zones by a defined, verifiable boundary — the unit E26's ship-level architecture requirements are built around.

Zone Boundary Protection

The controls (firewalling, access control, monitoring) applied at the point where one security zone connects to another, or to a network outside the ship.

Severity / Capability Category

The tier assigned to a computer-based system based on the consequence of its compromise, which in turn scales the cyber resilience capability the system must demonstrate.

Shipyard / System Integrator

The organization responsible for the vessel's overall network architecture and E26 compliance at delivery.

Equipment Manufacturer

The organization that designs and builds an individual computer-based system to E27's requirements before it is installed aboard.

Class Notation

The formal mark a classification society assigns to a vessel confirming it meets a given set of rules — including, for covered newbuilds, UR E26/E27 compliance.

07

Application & Practical Use

UR E26/E27 is applied at newbuild contract and delivery — it is not (by default) retroactive to vessels already in service, which is one of the most important distinctions to make clear to learners.

Newbuild Contracting

Applies to ships contracted for construction on or after 1 July 2024 — an owner ordering a new vessel today must specify E26/E27 compliance in the shipbuilding contract.

Shipyard Design Review

The yard's network architecture and each equipment maker's system design are reviewed against the requirements during construction, not only at final delivery.

Survey & Class Notation

A classification society surveyor verifies zone boundaries, remote access controls, and system-level hardening before issuing the class notation the vessel needs to trade.

Existing Fleet Gap

Vessels already in service are governed by the IMO's SMS-level expectation (MSC.428(98)) rather than UR E26/E27's technical design requirements — a real and frequently tested gap in maritime cyber governance.

08

Classroom Uses

Zone Diagram Redraw

Given a fictional vessel's system list (ECDIS, autopilot, ballast control, engine automation, crew Wi-Fi), learners group systems into security zones, draw the boundaries, and justify each boundary's protection level.

Capability Category Assignment

Learners assign a severity category to each system on the same vessel and identify which systems currently have insufficient capability for the category assigned — the newbuild-survey exercise in miniature.

Newbuild vs. In-Service Gap Analysis

Given two fictional vessels — one contracted in 2023, one in 2025 — learners identify which governance instrument applies to each and what that means for the owner's audit obligations.

Cross-Framework Bridge

Take the same zone assignments and map them to the IEC 62443 security-level table from Lesson 8's other framework page, showing where the two standards reinforce each other.
