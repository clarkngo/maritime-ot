IT Protocols Inside Shipboard & Port Networks

Ethernet, TCP/IP, VLANs, DHCP, DNS, and NTP as they actually appear on a vessel LAN and in a port terminal network — and why satellite backhaul and GNSS-derived time change how these get used.

📊 Intermediate ⏱ 4 hours 🎯 5 objectives
Overview Ethernet & TCP/IP VLAN Segmentation DHCP & DNS NTP & GNSS Time VSAT Backhaul Activity Simulation Review

Lesson Overview

By the time a modern vessel leaves the yard, its bridge, engine room, cargo systems, and crew accommodation are almost certainly wired with the same physical technology as a shore-side office: switched Ethernet carrying TCP/IP traffic. That is, in one sense, unremarkable — Ethernet and IP won the networking wars decades ago, on land and now afloat. What is easy to miss is that these familiar protocols do not behave identically on a ship the way they do in a building with a stable power grid, a wired internet uplink, and IT staff down the hall. A vessel is a mobile facility that is sometimes offline, that relies on a single expensive and latency-heavy satellite link for anything beyond the ship itself, and that increasingly derives its sense of time from the same satellite constellation it uses for navigation.

This lesson takes four pieces of everyday IT infrastructure — VLANs, DHCP, DNS, and NTP — and asks how each one is actually configured and used aboard ship and inside a port terminal network, where the assumptions of an always-online office LAN quietly stop applying. It closes with the constraint that shapes nearly every other design decision on a vessel network: the bandwidth, latency, and cost of the VSAT satellite link connecting that network to the rest of the world.

Anchoring analogy

A shore office's network assumes it can always reach the internet, always resolve any domain name, and always fetch a fresh IP address on demand — the way a house assumes the tap always has running water. A ship's network has to behave more like a boat with a fixed-size water tank: some things (DHCP leases, DNS lookups, patch downloads) that a shore network treats as free and unlimited have to be budgeted, cached, or done without for days at a time between refills.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain how Ethernet and TCP/IP underlie modern vessel and terminal networks, and where marine-specific standards like IEC 61162-450 fit alongside general IT networking.
  • Describe how VLAN segmentation is used (or should be used) aboard ship to separate navigation, engine control, cargo, and crew Wi-Fi traffic.
  • Explain how DHCP and DNS behave differently in an intermittently-connected vessel environment compared with a typical always-online office LAN.
  • Explain why accurate time synchronization matters aboard ship and how GNSS is commonly used as the master time source for NTP.
  • Describe VSAT satellite backhaul's bandwidth, latency, and cost constraints, and their security implications for patching, monitoring, and log shipping.

Ethernet and TCP/IP arrive on the bridge

≈ 30 min

Lesson 1 traced how marine electronics moved from point-to-point NMEA 0183 serial links onto shared Ethernet backbones under IEC 61162-450, the marine industry's own standard for carrying NMEA sentences as UDP multicast packets over standard switched Ethernet. What that lesson did not dwell on is that this shift meant the bridge's sensor network started running on exactly the same underlying technology stack — Ethernet at the physical/data-link layer, IP at the network layer, UDP or TCP at the transport layer — as the vessel's business LAN, its crew Wi-Fi, and, for that matter, the office network ashore. IEC 61162-450 did not invent a new kind of network; it defined how marine sensor data should ride on the network everyone else was already using.

That convergence at the protocol level is exactly why a modern newbuild's network diagram can look deceptively similar to an office building's: switches, routers, DHCP scopes, VLANs, and IP subnets, all speaking Ethernet and TCP/IP. The meaningful differences are not in the protocols themselves but in how they are configured — how tightly (or loosely) traffic is segmented, how addresses are assigned, and how much of the network design assumes the ship is reachable from shore at any given moment.

Worked example — flat vs. segmented newbuild network

Two sister vessels are delivered from the same yard in the same year. On Vessel A, the yard's integrator wired ECDIS, the engine alarm monitoring system, the cargo control system, and the crew Wi-Fi access points all onto one flat Ethernet network with no VLANs — every device can, in principle, address every other device, because nothing in the switch configuration says otherwise. On Vessel B, the same equipment sits behind a switch configured with separate VLANs per function, with routing between them restricted by an internal firewall. Both vessels are running identical Ethernet and TCP/IP hardware and protocols. The security-relevant difference is entirely in the segmentation choices layered on top — which is exactly the subject of Module 2.

Teaching note

Learners with an IT background often assume "it's just Ethernet and TCP/IP" means shipboard networking is basically solved. Use this module to set up the rest of the lesson: the protocols are familiar, but the physical environment (single point of shore connectivity, no always-on internet, a crew that is not IT staff) changes how those familiar protocols should be configured — which is a recurring theme through Modules 2 through 5.

VLANs as the first line of shipboard segmentation

≈ 35 min

A VLAN (Virtual LAN) is a way of dividing one physical switched network into several logically separate broadcast domains without running separate physical cabling for each one. A single switch chassis can carry navigation traffic, engine control traffic, cargo monitoring traffic, and crew Wi-Fi traffic on four different VLANs, each isolated from the others unless a router or firewall is deliberately configured to pass traffic between them. On a vessel where running fully separate physical networks to every deck would be expensive and heavy, VLANs are usually the first and cheapest segmentation tool available.

VLANs are not a substitute for a firewall — traffic within a VLAN is still fully open, and a misconfigured trunk port or an access port assigned to the wrong VLAN can undermine the whole scheme. But used correctly, a VLAN scheme gives a vessel network a documented, auditable answer to a basic security question: which systems are supposed to be able to reach which other systems, by design, before any firewall rule is even considered.

Anchoring analogy

A VLAN scheme is like a cruise ship deck plan that puts the bridge, the engine spaces, and the passenger decks on different levels connected only by controlled stairwells — everyone is still inside the same hull, but you cannot wander from the passenger deck straight onto the bridge without passing through a door someone has to deliberately open for you.

Worked example

A ferry operator discovers, during an audit, that its crew Wi-Fi access points are plugged into access ports configured for VLAN 10 — the bridge navigation VLAN — because the original installer ran out of ports on the switch reserved for VLAN 40 and used whatever was available. On paper, the vessel "has VLANs." In practice, any device joining crew Wi-Fi sits on the same broadcast domain as the ECDIS and autopilot. The lesson is not that VLANs failed; it is that a VLAN scheme is only as good as its ongoing configuration discipline.

DHCP and DNS in an intermittently-connected environment

≈ 30 min

In a typical office LAN, DHCP hands out IP addresses on demand and DNS resolves domain names by querying upstream servers over an always-available internet connection. Neither assumption holds unmodified aboard ship. Many OT devices — ECDIS units, radar processors, engine automation controllers — are configured with static IP addresses rather than DHCP-assigned ones. A static address means a device's address never changes unexpectedly, which matters when other systems, firewall rules, and documentation all reference that device by a fixed IP; the tradeoff is that reconfiguring a static address (moving a device to a new VLAN, for instance) is manual work rather than something DHCP would handle automatically.

DHCP still has a role aboard ship — it is the natural choice for the crew Wi-Fi VLAN, where transient personal devices join and leave constantly and predictability of any one address is not a design goal. DNS, meanwhile, often relies on local or cached resolution rather than live upstream queries, because a vessel can be entirely offline from the internet for stretches of a voyage — through a dead zone, a satellite outage, or simply because the ship's connectivity plan doesn't include always-on data. A shipboard network commonly runs its own internal DNS server (or relies on hosts-file style static entries) for names that must resolve locally regardless of whether the VSAT link is up.

Teaching note

Ask learners to imagine configuring a home office network that has to keep working correctly for three days if the internet modem is unplugged. Static addressing for anything that matters and local-only name resolution for critical services both start to look like obviously reasonable design choices rather than exotic maritime quirks once framed that way.

NTP, GNSS, and why time matters

≈ 35 min

NTP (Network Time Protocol) synchronizes device clocks across a network so that every system agrees, to within a small tolerance, on what time it is. Aboard ship this matters for reasons that go well beyond convenience. Alarm sequencing depends on accurate timestamps to establish which alarm fired first in a cascading failure. Log correlation across the bridge, engine room, and cargo systems depends on every device's clock agreeing closely enough that events from different systems can be lined up in the right order during an investigation. The Voyage Data Recorder (VDR) — the maritime equivalent of an aircraft's flight data recorder — depends on accurate timestamping to reconstruct the sequence of events before an incident.

Rather than relying on an internet-based NTP server that may be unreachable for days at a time, vessels commonly use a GNSS receiver as the master time source for shipboard NTP. GPS and other GNSS constellations broadcast an extremely accurate time signal as part of their normal operation — a signal the ship already receives continuously for navigation — so deriving network time from the same receiver is a natural and low-cost design choice that does not depend on the VSAT link being up. This convenience has a downside worth flagging now and returning to in Lesson 11: if the GNSS signal itself can be spoofed or jammed, the ship's entire sense of time — not just its position — is exposed to that same manipulation.

Worked example — a log correlation failure

During an incident investigation, analysts try to reconstruct the sequence of events leading to an engine trip. The bridge alarm log, sourced from a device syncing time from the ship's GNSS-derived NTP server, shows an alarm at 14:32:07. The engine control system's log, from a controller whose clock had drifted because its NTP client had silently failed months earlier, shows the same physical event at 14:29:41 — nearly three and a half minutes off. Investigators initially assume two separate events occurred, and lose time reconciling the timeline before realizing one clock was simply wrong. The underlying failure was not the alarm logic; it was an unmonitored clock drift that nobody had alerted on.

Teaching note

This is a good moment to preview Lesson 11's treatment of GNSS spoofing and time-sync integrity out loud: everything in this module assumes the GNSS time signal itself is trustworthy. That assumption is exactly what Lesson 11 challenges.

VSAT backhaul: bandwidth, latency, cost — and what that means for security

≈ 30 min

Nearly everything discussed so far — remote vendor patching, log shipping to a shore SOC, live dashboards, GNSS-independent NTP fallback — ultimately depends on the vessel's VSAT link, and that link is a much scarcer resource than most shore-side network designers assume. Depending on the plan and hardware, a vessel's total shared bandwidth might range from a few hundred kilobits per second up to a handful of megabits per second — shared across every system and every crew member aboard, all at once. Many plans also charge per megabyte transferred, which means a security control that silently ships large volumes of log data to shore can turn into an unexpectedly large invoice, not just a technical inconvenience.

Geostationary VSAT links also carry inherent latency: a signal traveling roughly 35,800 kilometers up to the satellite and back down, then the same distance in reverse for the reply, adds up to a round-trip time of 500 milliseconds or more before any processing delay is even counted. That latency does not just make a video call feel sluggish — it changes what "remote monitoring" or "remote diagnostics" can realistically mean. A shore-based analyst reacting to a live alarm feed over a half-second-plus delay is not operating in real time in the way an office-based IT helpdesk would assume.

Anchoring analogy

Think of a vessel's VSAT allocation the way you would think of a single household internet plan shared across every device in an entire apartment building — video streaming, video calls, downloads, and background updates all competing for the same modest pipe. On a ship, "the household" also includes the engine's telemetry upload, the OEM's remote diagnostic session, and the alarm log shipping to shore — all fighting the crew's Wi-Fi for the same limited, and often metered, connection.

The practical security implications follow directly from these constraints. Patch downloads have to be scheduled, batched, or delivered on removable media rather than pushed automatically the way an office IT team might push updates overnight. Remote monitoring and log shipping have to be designed around what fits in the available bandwidth budget without starving safety-critical traffic or blowing through a data cap — a design problem Module 5's simulation lets you experience directly.

Key Terms
  • VLANVirtual LAN — a logical segmentation of a physical switched network into separate broadcast domains, such as navigation, engine, cargo, and crew Wi-Fi, without requiring separate physical cabling.
  • DHCPDynamic Host Configuration Protocol — automatically assigns IP addresses to devices joining a network; common for crew Wi-Fi, less common for OT devices that favor static addressing.
  • NTPNetwork Time Protocol — synchronizes device clocks across a network; aboard ship, often sourced from a GNSS receiver to keep logs, alarms, and the VDR aligned to one accurate reference time.
  • VSATVery Small Aperture Terminal — the satellite dish and modem providing a vessel's primary broadband link to shore, typically constrained by limited bandwidth, high per-MB cost, and geostationary latency.
  • SegmentationThe practice of dividing a network into isolated zones, such as by VLAN, so that a compromise or fault in one zone does not automatically propagate to another.
