Capstone: Build and Teach Your Own Maritime OT Module

Produce a teachable module — objectives, one hands-on simulation, an assessment with a rubric, and a written safeguards statement — then teach a segment of it and take peer critique.

Lesson Overview

Eleven lessons in, you've now sat on the other side of a full maritime OT security curriculum — convergence, segmentation, protocols, threat actors, frameworks, governance, identity, and incident response, each delivered the same way: a lecture grounded in worked examples, a hands-on activity, a browser-based simulation, and a graded review. This capstone asks you to build one module of your own, in that same shape, on a maritime OT topic you choose — then teach a short excerpt of it to your peers and take their feedback.

This is deliberately a capstone project, not another content lecture: the four sections below are a project brief, not new subject-matter content to memorize. You will select a topic, write measurable objectives, design one interactive activity, draft an assessment with a scoring rubric, write a safeguards statement appropriate to your content, and rehearse a short taught segment — using the same component patterns (overview box, worked examples, mapping activity, toggle/meter simulation, graded quiz) every lesson on this site has already shown you how to build.

Anchoring analogy

Think of Lessons 1 through 11 as eleven cooking classes where you watched an instructor prepare a full dish from a recipe, step by step, explaining each technique as they went. This capstone is the moment you're handed your own set of ingredients — a maritime OT topic — and asked to write the recipe yourself: what technique does this dish actually need, in what order, and how will you know when it's done right. The techniques aren't new. Choosing which ones this specific dish calls for, and executing them cleanly, is the whole exercise.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Write clear, measurable learning objectives for a self-designed maritime OT module.
  • Design one hands-on simulation or activity appropriate to a chosen maritime OT topic.
  • Design an assessment item set with an accompanying scoring rubric.
  • Write a safeguards statement appropriate to the module's content.
  • Deliver a short taught segment and incorporate peer and instructor feedback.
Part A · Project Brief
🧭

Capstone overview and options

≈ 20 min

The deliverable is a single teachable module on a maritime OT security topic of your choosing, built to the same standard as Lessons 1 through 11: a stated set of learning objectives, structured lecture content, one hands-on interactive activity, an assessment with a rubric, and a written safeguards statement. You have two broad routes to a topic. You can go deeper on something this course already introduced but didn't fully unpack — a single IACS UR E26/E27 capability category from Lesson 8, a specific GNSS spoofing region from Lesson 6, the identity lifecycle problem from Lesson 10 applied to a fishing fleet instead of a merchant one. Or you can go adjacent — a maritime OT domain this course didn't cover at all, but that clearly belongs in the same family.

Either route is acceptable, and neither is automatically stronger than the other — a well-scoped deep dive on a narrow topic usually makes a better capstone module than a shallow survey of a broad new one. Whichever direction you choose, the topic needs to be narrow enough to teach in one module: not "maritime cybersecurity" but "OT security considerations specific to a container terminal's ship-to-shore cranes."

Example topic options
  • Port crane and container-terminal OT security — ship-to-shore crane control systems, terminal operating system integration, and gate automation.
  • Offshore platform control systems — drilling and production control networks, safety instrumented systems, and their exposure over supply-vessel and helicopter-linked connectivity.
  • Autonomous and remote-controlled vessel considerations — remote operations centers, the shift of "the bridge" ashore, and what that does to the access-control and IR material from Lessons 10 and 11.
  • Cruise ship hotel-service OT — HVAC, potable water treatment, and passenger-facing entertainment systems as a converged network sharing infrastructure with navigation-adjacent systems.
  • A deeper dive on a single IACS UR E26/E27 capability category that Lesson 8's orientation-level treatment only introduced.
  • Fishing or inland-waterway fleet OT security — applying this course's merchant-vessel-centric material to a smaller, less-resourced, less-regulated vessel class.
Teaching note

Have learners commit to a topic in writing (even a single sentence) before moving past this module — a capstone that drifts between two or three candidate topics through Modules 2 and 3 rarely converges into a coherent module by the end. Approval doesn't need to be formal; the point is forcing the choice early.

🎯

Writing learning objectives and structuring content

≈ 30 min

Every lesson on this site opens with a short list of objectives written using a small, deliberate set of verbs — Explain, Describe, Compare, Evaluate, Apply — because each one points to something a learner can actually demonstrate, rather than an internal state you'd have to take their word for. "Understand port crane cybersecurity" cannot be checked; "Explain why a ship-to-shore crane's control network is typically segmented from the terminal's business network" can be checked, directly, against what the learner writes or says. Your capstone module needs three to five objectives written the same way, each naming a verb from that set and a specific, checkable claim.

Weak versus measurable objective phrasing
Weak phrasingMeasurable rewrite
"Understand crane OT security.""Explain why a ship-to-shore crane's control network is typically segmented from the terminal's business IT network."
"Learn about offshore platform risk.""Describe the safety instrumented system boundary on an offshore platform and why it is treated as a separate security zone."
"Know about remote vessel operations.""Compare the access-control model of a traditional bridge crew to that of a shore-based remote operations center."
"Be aware of hotel-service OT.""Evaluate whether a cruise ship's HVAC and entertainment networks should share infrastructure with navigation-adjacent systems."

Once your objectives are set, structure your content the way Lessons 1 through 11 do: three to five content modules, each roughly 20 to 45 minutes, each built from a short lecture, at least one worked example grounding the concept in a concrete scenario, and — where it genuinely helps — an analogy box or comparison table. You do not need every component every lesson on this site uses; you need the ones that actually serve your topic. A module that forces in a timeline or a compare-table it doesn't need is weaker than one that skips it.

Teaching note

Push learners to draft their module outline — just headings and one sentence per module — before writing full prose. It's much cheaper to discover a five-module plan should really be three modules at the outline stage than after two of the five are fully written.

🧪

Designing one hands-on simulation

≈ 30 min

Every lesson in this course includes exactly one interactive activity beyond the mapping exercise and quiz, and it is always one of a small number of reusable mechanics — not because the course ran out of ideas, but because a small set of mechanics, reused well, teaches a learner the interaction pattern once and lets them focus on the content every time after. Your capstone's hands-on component should draw from the same small set: a toggle/meter simulation (like Lesson 1's isolation-erosion model or Lesson 11's correlation-confidence meter), where each toggle represents a choice or condition and a meter shows the cumulative effect; a mapping exercise (like the "match the X to its Y" activity every lesson includes), where learners pair scenarios to the correct concept; or a quiz with explanatory feedback, if your topic is better served by direct knowledge checks than by a simulated decision.

The design question that matters most is not which mechanic to pick — it's what single concept the mechanic should make concrete. A toggle/meter simulation works best when your topic has a clear cumulative or degrading quantity (isolation eroding, confidence building, accountability weakening) that toggles plausibly affect. A mapping exercise works best when your topic has five or six scenarios that cleanly sort into a small number of categories. If neither fits naturally, a well-written quiz with strong distractor options and real explanatory feedback is a completely legitimate choice — it is what several of this course's own review sections already are.

Worked example

A learner capstoning on port crane OT security considers three options: a toggle/meter simulation showing "terminal network isolation" eroding as remote diagnostics, TOS integration, and gate-camera feeds are added one at a time (directly parallel to Lesson 1's mechanic); a mapping exercise pairing crane subsystems (hoist control, anti-sway sensors, operator cab HMI) to their correct security zone; or a quiz on crane-specific failure scenarios. They choose the toggle/meter simulation, because their module's central claim — that a crane network's isolation erodes through the same kind of individually reasonable requests Lesson 1 described for vessels — is best demonstrated by watching a meter move, not by sorting items into categories.

Teaching note

Remind learners that the mechanic is reused, not the content — copying Lesson 1's four business-pressure toggles verbatim with a new label is not a capstone simulation. The toggles, the readout text, and the end-state message all need to be written fresh for the chosen topic.

Part B · Assessment, Delivery & Feedback
📝

Assessment design and rubric

≈ 25 min

Write three to five assessment items for your module, mirroring the quiz pattern used throughout this course: a clear stem, one correct answer, plausible distractors that reflect real misconceptions rather than obviously wrong filler, and a short explanatory feedback message for both the correct answer and the incorrect ones. Good distractors are the hardest part to get right — each wrong option should be something a learner who half-understood the material might actually pick, not a throwaway.

Pair your items with a simple four-criterion rubric so a peer or instructor reviewer — and you yourself, before submitting — can score the whole module consistently rather than on a vague overall impression.

Capstone module scoring rubric
CriterionDevelopingProficientExemplary
Technical accuracyContains factual errors or unsupported claims about maritime OT systems or security practiceTechnically accurate throughout, with claims that are reasonable and defensibleAccurate and precise, with worked examples that reflect real-world nuance and correct terminology
Safeguards framingNo safeguards statement, or one that doesn't match the module's actual contentIncludes a clear safeguards statement appropriate to the content and activity typeSafeguards statement is specific to the module's actual risks and reinforced within the activity itself
Activity designActivity is missing, generic, or only loosely connected to the module's core conceptActivity clearly reinforces one central concept using an appropriate, working mechanicActivity's mechanic, toggles/options, and end-state message are purpose-built to make the module's core claim concrete
Clarity of objectivesObjectives are vague, unmeasurable, or too broad for one moduleObjectives use measurable verbs and are scoped to what the module actually coversObjectives are precise, individually checkable, and each one is visibly addressed somewhere in the module content
Teaching note

Have learners self-score their own draft against this rubric before peer review. Most learners are harder on themselves for "activity design" than for "technical accuracy" — worth naming out loud, since a weak activity is usually more fixable in the time remaining than a technical gap is.

🎤

Teaching your segment and taking feedback

≈ 20 min

Prepare and deliver a 10 to 15 minute taught excerpt of your module to a small group of peers — not the whole module, just enough to demonstrate your objectives, one worked example, and your hands-on activity in action. Teaching a deliberately short excerpt forces the same prioritization discipline as writing the module itself: you cannot cover everything, so choose the segment that best demonstrates what makes your module work.

Structure the feedback you request from peers narrowly rather than opening the floor to general critique. Ask each peer for exactly three things: two things that worked and one suggestion. This constraint matters — open-ended critique tends to produce either polite vagueness or an unfocused list of every possible improvement, neither of which is actionable in the time available. Two-plus-one forces reviewers to commit to what was genuinely effective before offering a change, and gives you a short, prioritized list rather than a wall of notes.

Anchoring analogy

Unstructured critique is like being handed a marked-up manuscript with comments in every margin — technically thorough, but you don't know which three comments matter most until you've read all forty. "Two things that worked, one suggestion" is closer to a pit-stop debrief: fast, specific, and focused on what to change before the next lap, not a full performance review.

Simulation Design Worksheet

≈ 20 min

This lesson is about designing a simulation, not running one — so instead of a toggle/meter demo, work through the worksheet below for your own capstone topic. Answer each prompt in your own planning notes before you start building your activity in Module 3's mechanic.

📋

Worksheet: Planning your hands-on activity

Individual planning
Prompt 1

What single concept should your simulation make concrete? Name it in one sentence — if you can't, the activity likely isn't scoped tightly enough yet.

Prompt 2

What are three or four toggleable states, inputs, or scenario rows your activity needs? List them by name, the way Lesson 11's simulation lists "Alarm monitoring system log," "VDR data," "Network traffic capture," and "Synchronized accurate time source."

Prompt 3

What does the meter, readout, or feedback message communicate at each state? Draft the actual text a learner will read after each interaction — not just "meter goes up," but the sentence that explains why.

Prompt 4

Which existing course mechanic — toggle/meter simulation, mapping exercise, or quiz — fits this concept best, and why? Name the specific lesson whose mechanic you're modeling yours on.

Prompt 5

What happens if a learner selects every option? Does the end state teach something specific — the way Lesson 1's fully-converged network or Lesson 11's degraded-correlation warning does — or does it just say "done"? If it's the latter, revisit Prompt 1.
